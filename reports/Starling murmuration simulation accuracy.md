# Retuning the murmuration to measured starling physics

The most valuable changes to `murmuration.html` are not "more noise". They are a better measurement layer followed by four model changes. **First, the polarization gap is mostly a sampling artefact.** The app's Φ ≈ 0.99 sits inside the event-matched range of 0.957–0.997 that the Rome group measured at 0.2 s sampling, and the old pair of targets (Φ = 0.96 together with |u| ≈ 2 m/s) cannot both be met at 11.5 m/s. **Second, turning has to emerge rather than be scripted.** The app's waypoint "ripple" is hard-coded at 25 u/s (12.5 m/s), whereas real turns start at the flock's lateral tips and cross it linearly at **9.4–21.3 m/s (mean 15 ± 4 m/s)** with negligible attenuation. Getting that requires spin inertia plus a quartic (FPUT) alignment term, because spontaneous fluctuations in real flocks are overdamped. **Third, speed control should be marginal and per bird**, not a linear hold on the flock mean, and the 15 m/s cap should rise to about 19–20 m/s. **Fourth, the StarDisplay flight and roost layer** produces the thin 1 : 2.8 : 5.6 sheets, and an edge-biased cohesion rule produces the dense border. **Fifth, the falcon should become a shadow → proportional-navigation strike → retreat cycle**, and the starlings should answer with discrete, neighbour-copied escapes and roll ("zig") waves relayed at about 0.088 s per hop, not with an isotropic radial push. On criticality, the current reading is that orientation scale-freeness is a generic Goldstone effect, and speed scale-freeness follows from marginal confinement at low noise. Tuning to a critical point is contested both as a mechanism and as an adaptation. The app's own harmonic centring (W_GLOBAL) could manufacture ξ ∝ L, so it needs a control run. The OU noise with τ = 4 s has no empirical anchor: the measured orientation relaxation is about 0.2 s, and the app's alignment is roughly 2–8× stiffer than the data imply. Every change must preserve the one thing the app already does better than the best agent model, which is ξ/L ≈ 0.35.

Measurement items come first because they decide whether the later dynamics changes worked. Two invariants apply to every row. ## Six conflicting numbers, resolved before any code changes

The notes disagree in six places. Every one of the disagreements changes a target in the table above, so each is settled here with a stated value to trust.

| Conflict | Values in the notes | Value to trust | Deciding evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turn-propagation speed | 9.4–21.3 m/s vs "20–40 m/s" | **9.4–21.3 m/s, mean 15 ± 4 m/s** | Per-event table; the authors' own 2025 citation gives [10:20] m/s |
| Polarization | 0.96 at 10 fps; 0.957–0.997 at 0.2 s; 0.76–0.96 at 170 Hz | **All three, each tied to its sampling interval**; compare the app with 0.957–0.997 | Event-matched IDs show coarser sampling raises Φ by 0.03–0.21 |
| Aspect ratio | L₁/L₂ = 6–8 vs I₃/I₂ ≈ 2 | **I₂/I₁ = 2.8 ± 0.4, I₃/I₁ = 5.6 ± 1.0, hence I₃/I₂ ≈ 2** | Ballerini defines its axes; 6–8 only fits as longest/shortest |
| Wave copy delay | 0.043 vs 0.088 s | **0.088 s** | Arithmetic, plus the wave speed it predicts |
| Reaction time | 15, 50, 76 ms | **76 ms** for startle; 50 ms is a model update interval; drop 15 ms | Only 76 ms is a measurement |
| Φ–\|u\| tie | Φ = 0.96 needs \|u⊥\| ≈ 3.2 m/s | **Φ(0.2 s) ≈ 0.98–0.99 with \|u⊥\| ≈ 1.5–2 m/s** | 1 − Φ ≈ ⟨u⊥²⟩/2v² |

**Turn propagation: trust 9.4–21.3 m/s.** The arXiv table of Attanasi et al. lists 12 events at 170 Hz. Their c_s values are 10.09, 21.32, 16.19, 11.37, 11.93, 18.85, 19.23, 18.66, 17.70, 13.77, 9.41 and 10.98 m/s, a **mean of 15.0 m/s with SD 4.2** ([Attanasi 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097)). The paywalled journal table could not be read. However, the same group's 2025 preprint cites the published paper as "c_s ∈ [10:20] m/s" ([Cavagna 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19665)), which makes a material revision to 20–40 m/s implausible. The one note that gives "20–40 m/s" also concedes it could not find the companion "~50 ms" figure in the paper's text. Two explanations fit "20–40" without contradicting the table. It is exactly 10–20 m/s written in the app's world units (20–40 u/s), which is how the earlier canonical report states the target. It is also the upper half of the range implied by a/c_s = 25–100 ms with a ≈ 1 m (10–40 m/s). Both explanations are my inference, but either way the per-event numbers win. In app units the target is **19–43 u/s**, and it should be tested together with the rank-curve exponent (r ∝ t^3.2, i.e. a linear front).

**Polarization: three numbers, three sampling intervals.** Cavagna 2010 reports Φ = **0.96 ± 0.03** (24 flocks, range 0.844–0.995) from 10 fps velocities ([Cavagna 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.4393)). Attanasi 2014 reports **0.757–0.959** at 170 Hz, where wing-beat zig-zag is resolved ([Attanasi 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097)). Mora 2016 reports **0.957–0.997** at dt = 0.2 s ([Mora 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.01958)). The decisive evidence is that Mora's Table S1 reuses Attanasi's acquisition IDs. The same events go from 0.806 to 0.984, 0.757 to 0.971, 0.854 to 0.986, 0.959 to 0.993 and 0.841 to 0.957 as the sampling interval coarsens. That is a rise of 0.03–0.21 from resolution alone ([Mora 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.01958); [Attanasi 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097)). The app's Φ ≈ 0.99 comes from noise-free model velocities, so the matching comparison is the 0.2 s range (median ≈ 0.986), not 0.96. The canonical report's claim that the app is "too ordered" is substantially weakened. The app sits at the upper end of the field range, not outside it. Only a frame-rate Φ with simulated 10 Hz jitter should be compared with 0.76–0.96.

**Aspect ratio: trust Ballerini's three-axis numbers.** Ballerini et al. publish a per-flock table with the axes defined (I₁ thickness, I₃ longest). It gives I₂/I₁ = **2.8 ± 0.4** and I₃/I₁ = **5.6 ± 1.0**, so I₃/I₂ ≈ 2 ([Ballerini 2008a](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667)). Cavagna 2022 describes flocks as "quite elongated, with an aspect ratio L₁/L₂ which typically ranges between 6 and 8" ([Cavagna 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748)). That is irreconcilable with I₃/I₂ ≈ 2 unless L₂ means the shortest axis. Read that way, it matches Ballerini's per-flock I₃/I₁ range of **4.02–8.36**. The 2022 axis definition was not confirmed in the notes, so 6–8 should be quoted only as longest/shortest and never used as a long/middle target.

**Agitation-wave copy delay: trust 0.088 s.** Hemelrijk et al. print the mean repeat delay as "0.05 s + 0.076/2 = 0.043 s", but the sum is 0.088 s ([Hemelrijk 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3)). The physics agrees with the arithmetic. At their NND of 1.1–1.3 m, a 0.088 s hop gives **12.5–14.8 m/s**, matching the field mean of **13.4 m/s** (range 3.66–25.24) ([Procaccini 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2011.07.006), as quoted by Hemelrijk 2015). A 0.043 s hop would give 26–30 m/s, faster than any recorded wave.

**Reaction times: 76 ms is the only measurement.** Pomeroy & Heppner timed starling startle at **76.38 ms to light and 80.64 ms to sound** in an anechoic chamber ([Pomeroy & Heppner 1977](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/000334727790121X)). StarDisplay and StarEscape use 0.076 s (SD 0.01) for that reason ([Hemelrijk 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3)). The 50 ms figure has two sources, and neither is a latency measurement. One is StarDisplay's steering re-evaluation interval Δu ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). The other is Attanasi's mean a/c_s ≈ 50 ms, which the authors explicitly say is not a reaction time ([Attanasi 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097)). The 15 ms "chorus-line" figure could not be traced. It most likely comes from Potts' dunlin work ([Potts 1984](https://doi.org/10.1038/309344a0)) and has no starling support, so drop it. The app should carry two latencies. One is ≈ 76 ms (plus 50 ms cue identification) for copying alarms and escapes. The other is a slower effective lag for routine heading-following, about **0.32–0.37 s** in homing pigeons ([Nagy 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08891)).

**The Φ–|u| tie: the old targets are inconsistent, and the fix is a new pair.** For small transverse fluctuations, 1 − Φ ≈ ⟨u⊥²⟩/(2v²) ([Cavagna 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19665)). At 11.5 m/s, Φ = 0.96 requires |u⊥| ≈ **3.2 m/s rms**, while |u⊥| = 2 m/s gives Φ ≈ 0.985. The app obeys this geometry: its measured |u| of 1.0–1.5 m/s predicts Φ ≈ 0.992–0.996 if the fluctuation is transverse, and it measures 0.99–1.00 (my arithmetic). Two observations dissolve the conflict. Φ = 0.96 is a 10 fps number inflated by measurement noise. The provenance of the "|u| ≈ 2 m/s" target is also doubtful: Cavagna 2022's "typical fluctuations of 2 m/s" describes the *flock-to-flock* spread of group speed, not within-flock fluctuation ([Cavagna 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748)). The consistent replacement uses Mora's median Φ(0.2 s) ≈ 0.986, which at 11.5 m/s implies |u⊥| ≈ **1.9 m/s**. The target pair is therefore Φ(0.2 s) ≈ 0.98–0.99 with |u⊥| ≈ 1.5–2 m/s, plus whatever longitudinal (speed) fluctuation marginal confinement adds. Speed fluctuation raises |u| without lowering Φ, because Φ averages unit vectors. Bialek et al.'s neighbour mismatch Q_int ~ 10⁻² ([Bialek 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1324045111)) corresponds to rms neighbour velocity differences of about 0.14 v₀ ≈ 1.6 m/s (my arithmetic), which supports the same order of magnitude.

**Corrected author lists.** The canonical report's list for Cavagna et al. 2022 wrongly includes Jelić and Parisi and omits Kion-Crosby. The PubMed record (PMID 35538068) gives **Cavagna A, Culla A, Feng X, Giardina I, Grigera TS, Kion-Crosby W, Melillo S, Pisegna G, Postiglione L, Villegas P**. The paper is "Marginal speed confinement resolves the conflict between correlation and control in collective behaviour", *Nat Commun* 13:2315 ([doi:10.1038/s41467-022-29883-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29883-4)). The Comment arXiv:1912.07056 on Casiulis et al. is by **Cavagna A, Giardina I and Viale M** (submitted 15 Dec 2019, single version), not by Cavagna, Giardina, Grigera, Mora and Walczak. It carries no journal reference, and no published PRL Comment was found ([arXiv:1912.07056](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07056)). The original was published as *PRL* 124:198001 on 15 May 2020 ([arXiv:1911.06042](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06042)). The reply is [arXiv:1912.09202](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09202).

## Criticality survives as Goldstone order plus marginal speed, not tuning

The 2010 claim that flocks "behave as critical systems" rested on **ξ ∝ L for both orientation (0.35 L) and speed (0.36 L)**, with a decay exponent **γ = 0.19 ± 0.08** that is statistically compatible with zero ([Cavagna 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.4393)). The Rome group has since split that claim in two. Orientation correlations are scale-free because the flock has broken a continuous symmetry. That is a Goldstone mode, generic throughout the ordered phase, and needs no tuning ([Bialek 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1107.0604)). Speed is a modulus with no Goldstone protection. Its scale-free correlation was first read as an individual speed stiffness tuned near zero, g/(Jn_c) ~ 10⁻³ ([Bialek 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1324045111)). It is now explained by a **marginal confinement potential V ∝ (v² − v₀²)⁴**. That potential has zero curvature at v₀ for any strength, so long-range speed correlation follows from low noise alone, approaching a zero-temperature critical point ([Cavagna 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748)). Φ > 0.95 places starlings "deep in the ordered phase", which the same paper says rules out proximity to an ordering transition. Turning is a third, separate story. Linear, unattenuated propagation needs behavioural inertia ([Attanasi 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097)). The 2025 spectra show that small spontaneous fluctuations are overdamped while large turns travel as solitons ([Cavagna 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19665)).

Agent models reach the same place without any tuning. StarDisplay produces ξ ∝ L with slopes **a_u = 0.44 (n = 344, r = 0.98) and a_sp = 0.41 (n = 340, r = 0.96)** using rules inspired by biology and weak per-bird speed control, and its authors do not claim criticality ([Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2015](https://research.rug.nl/files/17747148/Hemelrijk_2015_JStatPhys.pdf)). The slope rises with the number of influential neighbours, with diminishing returns. At very stiff speed control (w_sp = 100 s⁻¹) the speed correlation stops being scale-free and flocks begin to break up. This is a ready-made negative test for the app.

The critiques are substantive, and none has been rebutted in print. Klamser & Romanczuk show that, in a spatial predator–prey model, individual selection pushes alignment deep into the ordered phase (ESS μ ≈ 4.4 against a critical μ_c ≈ 0.9). That makes the critical point an evolutionary repeller ([Klamser & Romanczuk 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008832)). Golden-shiner schools measured by startle cascades are **subcritical**. Collective sensitivity rises from 0.035 to 0.058 under alarm, and the schools could raise it 5.9× and 3.4× by becoming critical, but they do not ([Poel 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9217090/)). The field now speaks of "tuning to an optimal distance from criticality" ([Romanczuk et al. review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03879)). Casiulis et al. proposed rigid rotations of a Hamiltonian droplet as a simpler source of bird-like correlations. Cavagna, Giardina and Viale reply that starlings turn on equal-radius paths, that g(r) is gas-like, and that **subtracting a Kabsch-fitted rigid rotation leaves the scale-free correlations essentially unchanged** ([arXiv:1912.07056](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07056)). Information-theoretic meters are unreliable: in finite Vicsek flocks, global transfer entropy stays flat across the whole ordered regime rather than peaking at the transition ([Sci Rep 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7052242/)). Two preprints show that critical-like signatures can arise from simpler ingredients: non-reciprocity alone gives constant-speed turn propagation ([arXiv:2511.17804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17804)), and minority-following gives heavy-tailed cascades with no fine-tuning ([arXiv:2603.07254](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07254)). Two gaps also remain open. No new starling γ has been measured since 2010; the 2022 extension to N = 10–3000 reports ξ_sp ∝ L but no γ ([Cavagna 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29883-4)). The boundary-inflow explanation of γ ≈ 0 ([Cavagna 2013 PRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.6314)) is still untested.

The critique that bites the app hardest is "scale-free chaos". In a harmonically confined Vicsek model, a critical line of confinement against noise produces ξ ∝ swarm size and power laws without any Goldstone mechanism ([González-Albaladejo et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08121); [Bonilla & González-Albaladejo 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10242)). The app gives every bird a harmonic pull toward the flock centre (`W_GLOBAL·dx`, 0.12 s⁻², line 394), plus a rejoin force beyond R_FLOCK and a roost-like target drive. These are exactly that ingredient. The Rome group notes that real starlings over a roost break rotational symmetry spontaneously, unlike jackdaws or pigeons flying to a fixed goal ([Cavagna 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748)). Priority 6 therefore requires a control run with W_GLOBAL = W_TGT = 0, under periodic or very weak confinement. If ξ/L falls sharply in that run, the app's scale-freeness is partly confinement-made. Three more checks complete the battery: the Kabsch subtraction, with ξ/L expected to change by less than a few percent; a K scan from 2 to 25, with ξ/L expected to rise and saturate; and a susceptibility proxy against N, which should grow monotonically (Goldstone) rather than peak in noise. The app should not be validated with transfer entropy. A further prediction from massive fish-school models is that **ξ/L drops before fragmentation** ([arXiv:2505.05822](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05822)), so logging ξ/L just before falcon-induced splits is a cheap extra test.

## Four competing models each own one slice of the data

No published model reproduces every starling observable, and no paper benchmarks the model families head-to-head on one dataset. **StarDisplay** is the only model fitted trait by trait to the Rome flocks. Against 10 flocks it passes **10 of 18 quantitative tests**: aspect ratios, density independent of N, a right-skewed NND distribution, bearing anisotropy, and I₁ perpendicular to the flight direction. It fails on the denser border (model NND 1.28 m at the border against 1.24 m in the centre, the opposite sign to the data), on volume and thickness (thickness 6.6 m against 19.0 m in flock 31-01), and on altitude variability ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). Its ingredients are useful to borrow: about 6.5 topological neighbours, Gaussian separation with a 0.2 m hard core, a 2 × 45° rear blind angle for alignment and cohesion only, banking with roll-in gain w_βin = 10 against roll-out w_βout = 1, a vertical roost spring of 0.2 N on an 80 g bird, a 150 m roost radius, a 50 ms update and a 5 ms step. Its speed law should not be borrowed. StarDisplay uses linear per-bird relaxation to 10 m/s with τ = 1 s, and its own 2015 paper shows that stiffer speed control shrinks ξ_sp.

The **inertial spin model** gives each bird a spin s ⟂ v with inertia χ and friction η. In the underdamped regime a turn crosses the flock at c_s = a√(n_c J/χ) without attenuation, and the static properties equal Vicsek's ([Cavagna 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.1202)). Adding the ISM therefore changes turning but not Φ or C(r) at fixed noise. The **ISM+FPUT** extension adds a quartic alignment J₄ inside the window J ≲ χγ²(L/a)² ≪ J₄ ≲ J/(1−Φ). Small misalignments then relax diffusively and large ones travel as solitons, matching both the Lorentzian spectra and the linear turns ([Cavagna 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19665)). **Marginal speed confinement** is the only model that fits the single-bird speed distributions at three flock sizes. Linear control is "a total disaster" at medium and small N, and it fits scale-free ξ_sp and an N-independent mean speed only at contradictory stiffnesses ([Cavagna 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748)).

**Marginal opacity** steers each bird toward the light/dark boundaries in its view of the flock. It holds projected opacity at an N-independent intermediate value, matching UK photographs (Θ′ mean **0.30**, n = 118; public images 0.41; range 0.25–0.6) ([Pearce 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.2414)). Three problems keep it out of the core model. It predicts ρ ∝ N^(−1/2) in 3-D, against Ballerini's density independent of N. It is O(N²) per step. It has never been tested in 3-D. The opacity *meter* is cheap and should be added. A follow-up metric-free model with an inward bias for edge birds and an outward bias for interior birds fits the denser border ([Pearce & Turner](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08181)), and that bias is the mechanism Priority 8 borrows.

The composite these results point to is: the app's existing K = 7 topological alignment; per-bird marginal speed; ISM+FPUT turning; the StarDisplay flight, roost and separation geometry; and the Pearce–Turner border bias, with Θ′ as a diagnostic. This is a synthesis and has not been tested anywhere as a single model. Its pieces are each tested against data. The data-driven inference literature points the same way: equations inferred from bird trajectories come out "remarkably" similar to a second-order (inertial) Vicsek model with white noise ([Gao 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11254936/)).

## Turns must emerge at 9–21 m/s from inertia, not from a scripted ripple

The app currently turns the flock by moving a target. Each bird's response is delayed by `lag = 1.2 − (projection on the old heading)/25` (line 410), so the turn is a pre-programmed front sweeping backward at **25 u/s = 12.5 m/s**. That speed falls inside the empirical range, so any meter will "pass" by construction. The geometry is also wrong. Real spontaneous turns start among a few birds at the **lateral tips of the long axis**: the ten first-ranked birds have a median |I₃·d̂₀| of 0.91 and are not at the front. Those birds deviate *persistently* rather than strongly, and the flock turns *away* from the initiating edge in 9 of 12 events ([Attanasi 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330)). The fix comes in two steps. First, measure c_s with the scripted lag set to zero, or with a heading impulse given only to tip birds. Rank birds by peak low-pass-filtered radial acceleration, convert rank to distance as x = (rank/ρ)^(1/3), and fit the linear regime against **9.4–21.3 m/s** with a/c_s of **25–100 ms**. Second, before adding inertia, check whether the app's already non-reciprocal K = 7 rule gives a linear front (r ∝ t^≈3) by itself, as a 2025 preprint argues non-reciprocity can ([arXiv:2511.17804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17804)). If the front is diffusive (r ∝ t^1.5) and attenuated, which first-order alignment predicts, add the spin layer.

**Spin-layer parameters in physical units** follow from the data by conversion (my inference from the ISM relations). With an interaction distance a ≈ 1.2–1.5 m and c_s = 10–20 m/s, √(n_c J/χ) = c_s/a ≈ 7–17 s⁻¹. The underdamped condition requires the spin damping time χ/η to be much longer than the crossing time L/c_s, which is about **2–3 s for L = 30–40 m** ([Cavagna 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.1202)). The app's 4 s OU time constant already sits on that scale. The FPUT window allows J₄/J ≲ 1/(1−Φ), which is about 25 at Φ = 0.96 and about 70–100 at Φ = 0.986–0.99. A scan of **J₄/J from 5 to 100** brackets it. The paper's own J₄/J = 5000 works only at its near-zero noise and should not be copied ([Cavagna 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19665)). At 60 fps the fastest mode, ω_max ≈ 2c_s/a ≈ 25 rad/s, gives ω·dt ≈ 0.4, which is stable under symplectic Euler. Two substeps add margin.

**Equal-radius turning is the shape-level test of the same physics.** In all 12 Attanasi events, birds keep |v| nearly constant and follow similar curvature radii around *different* centres, so trajectories cross. The mean rotation of inter-bird vectors θ_r stays small while the flock velocity rotates by θ_V; a rigidly rotating flock would have θ_r = θ_V ([Attanasi 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330)). Because the flock's absolute orientation barely changes, its long axis goes from roughly perpendicular to V before a turn (median |I₃·V| = **0.32**) to aligned after it (median **0.895**). The original side birds end at the front or back. In rock-dove flocks **75.6 ± 12.5%** of birds change quadrant in a turn ([Pomeroy & Heppner 1992](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/auk/vol109/iss2/6/)). Spontaneous turns last **1.8–12.9 s** ([Attanasi 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330)) at flock speeds of 7–12 m/s ([Attanasi 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3035)). The flock plane also banks into the turn: I₁ tilts away from G toward the turn ([Ballerini 2008a](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667)). Low speed variability is what makes this happen. In the same group's models, raising speed CV from 0.05 to 0.20 raised elongation along V from about 1.1 to 3.5 and suppressed position swapping ([Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0022479)). An app flock persistently elongated along V would therefore mean speed is too adjustable.

**Speed control should move from the flock mean to the bird.** The app's speed term is `W_SPD·(CRUISE − mean speed) + W_SPD4·ds³` (line 428). In physical units the per-bird cubic is only about 0.008·ds³ m/s², i.e. 0.06 m/s² at a 2 m/s deviation (my arithmetic). Individual speed is therefore set almost entirely by alignment, by the hard clamps at 6 and 15 m/s, and by a flock-mean hold that no real bird can sense. Cavagna's marginal force, **a = (8λ/v₀⁶)·v·(v₀² − |v|²)³**, acts per bird and has zero linear stiffness at v₀ ([Cavagna 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748)). Near v₀ it reduces to a pure cubic, −64λ(v − v₀)³/v₀², so it needs a clamp or substeps for explicit-Euler stability. The pass criterion is that mean speed stays within **12 ± 2 m/s with no trend in N** across N = 20–3000 *without* the global hold. Small flocks speeding up would reveal the entropic 1/√(Ng) drift that sinks linear control. ξ_sp/L should stay ≈ 0.36. StarDisplay's per-bird linear law at 1 s⁻¹ gives ξ_sp/L = 0.41, so it is worth keeping as a `?speed=stardisplay` comparison arm ([Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2015](https://research.rug.nl/files/17747148/Hemelrijk_2015_JStatPhys.pdf)). The **15 m/s cap is too low**. Mean speeds across 21 Rome flocks were **8.1–19.2 m/s** (median about 11.2), with one at 19.17 m/s and three at 14.0–14.4 m/s, where individual birds would hit the clamp ([Bialek 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1324045111)). Cavagna 2010 reports centre-of-mass speeds up to 19.1 m/s ([Cavagna 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.4393)). A VMAX of 19–20 m/s, a cruise of 10–12 m/s and the existing VMIN of 6 m/s are all supported.

## Individual starlings fly at 10 Hz, turn under 1 g and react in 76 ms

The single-bird envelope mostly confirms the app's constants. **Cruise is right.** Wind-tunnel kinematics place starling minimum-power speed near **12 m/s** ([Tobalske 1995](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.198.6.1259)), and respirometry shows sustained flight from **6.3 to 14.4 m/s** at 10.4–14.9 W ([Ward 2001](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.204.19.3311)). Migrants fly faster, at 17.3 ± 1.75 m/s airspeed ([Pennycuick 2001](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.204.19.3283)), which supports a faster transit mode for arriving flocks. **The 8 m/s² cap is right for routine turns.** No direct starling load factor exists. The best proxy is jackdaws (about 2.5–3× a starling's mass), whose peak centripetal acceleration in collective turns stayed "nearly constant at about 7 m s⁻²" while they slowed to tighten turns ([Ling 2019](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2019.0450)). At 8 m/s² and 11.5 m/s the minimum turn radius is about 16.5 m and a 90° turn takes about 2.2 s (my arithmetic), consistent with Attanasi's turn durations. Flock pigeons briefly pull about 2 g in banked turns ([Usherwood 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10164)). That bounds an escape-mode lateral acceleration of about 15–17 m/s². The app's alarm cap, 8 × (1 + 1.3) = 18.4 m/s², is in that range. It should be time-limited to a few tenths of a second rather than held for as long as alarm persists. StarDisplay's escape manoeuvres use a 0.25 s sideways zig, a 0.30 s return and a 1.0 s refractory period ([Hemelrijk 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3)).

**Three visual details are off.** First, free-flying starlings beat their wings at **10.2 ± 0.86 Hz** ([Pennycuick 2001](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.204.19.3283)), and the 2025 tracking shows a 68 rad/s (≈ 10 Hz) peak ([Cavagna 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19665)). The app's per-bird 7–11 Hz (line 256) should be re-centred to about 9–12 Hz. Second, starlings never flap continuously. They alternate flapping with glides at low speed and with bounds (wings folded) at high speed, and the flapping share is highest at 8 m/s ([Tobalske 1995](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.198.6.1259)). A two-state animation whose duty cycle falls with speed, with flap bursts tied to acceleration, would be more faithful. Third, the bank formula `atan(lat/9)` (line 478) uses lateral acceleration in u/s². The coordinated-turn bank is atan(a/g) with g = 19.6 u/s², so the app banks with about 2.2× the coordinated tangent: about 61° rather than 39° at 8 m/s² (my arithmetic). Because the visible dark bands of agitation waves are a bank-angle effect (Priority 11), bank should become physically correct, or the exaggeration should be a deliberate, documented choice. StarDisplay's roll-in/roll-out asymmetry (10 : 1, tuned to Rome video) should replace the single 6 s⁻¹ relaxation ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). Pigeon turns show that body rotation leads the path change by about one wing beat ([Ros 2011](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1107519108)). The more faithful causal order is therefore that bank produces lateral acceleration, not that lateral acceleration sets bank.

**Perception and latency.** Starlings have a maximum binocular overlap of **43°**, and their rear blind sector varies by up to 42° with eye position ([Martin 1986](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00604174)). This makes StarDisplay's 90° rear blind angle for alignment and cohesion a modelling choice that is probably larger than anatomy. Small passerines resolve flicker at 128–146 Hz ([Boström 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0151099)), so visual sampling is far faster than any reaction time and can be ignored. The latency architecture follows from the reconciliation above. Use about 76 ms (plus 50 ms cue identification) for startle copying, which is about 5 frames at 60 fps. Use an effective routine alignment lag of order 0.2–0.3 s. The app's W_ALI = 18 s⁻¹ is a 56 ms time constant (doubled under alarm), several times faster than either the pigeon following lag ([Nagy 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08891)) or the starling orientation relaxation of about 0.2 s ([Mora 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.01958)).

## A flat, sparse sheet with a dense rim: shape, density and internal structure

Undisturbed Rome display flocks are **thin horizontal sheets**. Their proportions are I₁ : I₂ : I₃ = 1 : 2.8 : 5.6, independent of N and speed. The thickness of 5.3–19 m scales with volume^(1/3) (R² = 0.97, n = 10). Orientation statistics are |I₁·G| = 0.93 ± 0.04, |V·G| = 0.13 ± 0.05 and |V·I₁| = 0.19 ± 0.08, and there is no fixed relation between the long axis and the velocity ([Ballerini 2008a](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667)). With r₁ ≈ 1 m, a 5–8 m thickness is only 5–8 birds deep. The app's flock-mean levelling (`W_LEVEL·gvy`) and halved vertical noise flatten the flock, but nothing sets its thickness. StarDisplay shows that a **per-bird vertical spring to a preferred height** (0.2 N on 80 g, about 2.5 m/s² per metre) is what flattens the flock. Without the spring, thickness grows markedly. Without banking, the flock circles the roost edge and becomes oblong ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). The diagnostics are I₁, I₂/I₁ and I₃/I₁ from PCA every second. A further check is the angle between I₃ and V over a long run, whose distribution should be broad rather than peaked at 0°. Under falcon attack, transient vertical "columnar" elongation is realistic rather than a bug ([Papadopoulou 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4)).

**Density and spacing.** Nearest-neighbour distance runs **0.68–1.51 m** across 10 flocks (1.4–3.0 u). Density runs 0.04–0.80 m⁻³ and is independent of N (P = 0.39) and of speed. A hard-sphere fit gives **r_h = 0.19 ± 0.02 m**, so P(r₁) vanishes below about 0.38 m, one wingspan, with packing fraction < 0.012 ([Ballerini 2008a](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667)). The app's separation zone, RS = 2.5 m, is about 6.5× the hard core. It acts as personal space rather than a core. In StarDisplay's Gaussian form, r_sep = 2.4–3.0 m maps to NND ≈ 0.9–1.1 m ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). If the app's r₁ lands well above 1.5 m at RS = 5 u, separation is too strong. Measure r₁ on internal birds only, because border birds inflate it. **Neighbours avoid the direction of motion.** In every flock the nearest neighbour is depleted along V: P(cosθ ≈ ±0.9) = 0.355–0.437 against 0.51–0.58 near cosθ = 0. The anisotropy factor γ(1) ≈ 0.70–0.73 decays to the isotropic 1/3 between the 6th and 7th neighbour ([Ballerini 2008b](https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.1916)). StarDisplay reproduces this (Kendall τ = 0.72) only with omnidirectional separation and a rear blind angle confined to alignment and cohesion. Putting a blind angle on separation breaks the fit (τ = −0.11) ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)).

**The border is denser than the core, and most models get this backwards.** r₁ rises from border to interior by about 20–50%: 1.00 → 1.49 m, 1.03 → 1.58 m, 0.64 → 0.78 m and 0.79 → 0.96 m in four flocks. A Poisson control with the same border stays flat ([Ballerini 2008a](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667)). StarDisplay, despite boosting cohesion for border birds, fails with the opposite sign ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). The candidate fix is the Pearce–Turner bias: inward for birds with an empty half-space and slightly outward for deep interior birds ([Pearce & Turner](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08181)). Topological interactions alone also lengthen border residence and sharpen borders ([Reynolds 2025](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2025.0020)). Measure r₁ in 0.5 m depth bins from a convex-hull or α-shape border. The target is r₁(core)/r₁(border) ≈ 1.2–1.5. The centre-of-mass offset along V (balance shift) should hover between −0.1 and +0.5 with either sign. A neighbour-count dimension d̂ ≈ 2.3 (flock 69-10) checks that the flock is not too spherical ([Cavagna 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434)).

**Internal motion is super-diffusive, anisotropic and slow to reshuffle.** In the centre-of-mass frame, birds have MSD ∝ t^α with **α = 1.73 ± 0.07** over 0.4–1.5 s lags (six flocks). Diffusion is fastest along the wing axis (α = 1.78), slower along V (1.63) and slowest vertically (1.44), with coefficients ordered roughly 5 : 2 : 1. Mutual diffusion is about 3× smaller than CoM diffusion, because correlated neighbours drift together. "It takes about 3.5 seconds to change only half of 10 neighbours", and border birds stay on the border for about **2.5 s against 0.8 s** predicted by diffusion ([Cavagna 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434)). StarEscape, tuned to these data, gives Q(3 s) = 0.58 ± 0.06 and NND = 0.86 ± 0.08 m at 9.5 m/s. It matches the diffusion coefficient only when each bird avoids its *single* nearest neighbour rather than six or seven ([Papadopoulou 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4); [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0126913)). If the app's neighbour overlap proves too sticky, applying separation to the nearest neighbour only is the published remedy. The absolute diffusion coefficient has ambiguous units in the source, so α and the axis ordering are the trustworthy targets.

## Behavioural noise: the 4-second OU process has no empirical anchor

The app drives each bird with an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck acceleration of stationary σ = 9 u/s² (4.5 m/s²) per axis and τ = **4 s** (line 224). The source comment explains why: with τ = 0.5 s and W_ALI = 4.5, the correlations died beyond 0.15 L. The evidence for that choice is thin. Every model fitted to starling or pigeon trajectories uses **white noise**. That includes Mora's dynamical maximum entropy (overdamped, white, τ_relax "of the same order" as 0.2 s) ([Mora 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.01958)), the marginal-speed simulations ([Cavagna 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748)), the ISM and FPUT models, whose memory comes from inertia χ ([Cavagna 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.1202)), and the pigeon equation inferred by sparse regression, which is second order with additive white noise ([Gao 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11254936/)). Cavagna 2022 lists correlated noise only as an untested alternative. StarDisplay reaches high order with a white random force of 0.01 N on 80 g, about 0.125 m/s² ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). The app's noise amplitude is therefore roughly 36× larger, persistent, and balanced by stiff alignment. That is a different regime, not a calibrated one (my comparison).

Two numbers make the mismatch concrete. Using Mora's relation D_eff = J n_c a² ≈ a²/τ_relax with a = 0.62–1.24 m and τ_relax ≈ 0.2 s gives an orientation diffusion constant of about **2–8 m²/s**. The app's own comment gives D ≈ W_ALI·3.5 u² ≈ 63 u²/s ≈ **15.8 m²/s** (inference from [Mora 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.01958)). The 2025 spectra provide a direct discriminator. Spontaneous C(k,ω) is a Lorentzian of half-width ω_L = Dk² ([Cavagna 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19665)). An OU process with τ = 4 s injects power at ω ≲ 0.25 rad/s, which would show as excess low-frequency weight beyond that Lorentzian (inference). Some persistence is real, though. Turn initiators are distinguished by *how persistently* they deviate, over seconds ([Attanasi 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330)). Pigeon pairwise heading correlations stay ≥ 0.8 at ±2 s ([Nagy 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08891)). Jackdaw models add a velocity-memory timescale, i.e. an OU velocity driven by white noise ([Reynolds 2025](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2025.0020)). OU noise of a few seconds also plausibly produces the α ≈ 1.7 super-diffusion, but that is untested.

The recommendation is a staged A/B test rather than a swap. Keep OU τ = 4 s as the default until the spin layer exists, since removing it now would break ξ/L. Then add `?noise=white` with spin inertia (χ/η ≈ 3–8 s) and lower W_ALI toward the data-implied ≈ 5 s⁻¹. Judge the two arms on six targets: ξ/L ≈ 0.35, a single-bird heading autocorrelation (flock frame) decaying on a scale of about 0.2 s, a Lorentzian C(k,ω) with D in the 2–8 m²/s band, MSD α in 1.6–1.85 with the lateral > along-V > vertical ordering, D_m/D ≈ 0.1–0.45, and Q₁₀(3.5 s) ≈ 0.5. Persistent noise should be kept where the data put it, at the elongated tips of the flock, as a position-dependent term that seeds spontaneous turns. One methodological caution applies when fitting the app's own trajectories: naive finite-difference inference of second-order models fails at any sampling rate ([arXiv:1912.10491](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10491)).

## Rebuild the falcon as shadow, proportional-navigation strike and retreat

The current falcon flies pure pursuit at **16 m/s** with a 0.35 s lead and a 27.5 m/s² (2.8 g) cap. It retargets the nearest bird every 1.2–1.8 s and leaves after 9 s of attack (lines 283–317). Three things are wrong with this against the data. The guidance law is wrong. The falcon is slower than alarmed starlings, whose cap rises to 19.5 m/s. And the sortie compresses into 9 s what real hunts spread over a minute or more. Captive peregrines' terminal attacks follow **proportional navigation**, with turn rate = N × line-of-sight rate, a median **N = 2.6** and a global optimum of 3.0. The fitted terminal phase lasts a median **4.9 s**. The line of sight holds within ±3° over the last 1–2 s, and centripetal acceleration stays below 2.5 g until the bird is within striking distance ([Brighton 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1714532114)). Theory and gyrfalcon data put the optimum near N ≈ 3 in a stoop and lower (about 1.5–2.5) in a tail chase on an evasive target ([Brighton 2021](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.238493)). Only **4 of 12** live hunts began with a stoop. Peregrines' maximum sustained level speed is **28–29 m/s** ([Mills 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jav.01979)). The fastest measured falcon dives reach **52–58 m/s** at 17–62° dive angles, followed by braking at about 1 g ([Tucker 1998](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.201.13.2061)). Against erratically jinking starling-like prey, simulated catch success peaks with high stoops that arrive at over 100 m/s ([Mills 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1006044)).

The StarEscape predator gives the cycle structure. It targets the largest flock, shadows it from behind at a set distance while matching its heading, strikes at **1.3× the target's speed** toward the closest bird for a fixed time, retreats, and repeats ([Papadopoulou 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4)). Falcon speed near wave-generating flocks was 11–15 m/s ([Hemelrijk 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3), citing Procaccini's table). The app's falcon should therefore shadow at about 11–15 m/s (22–30 u/s), then strike under PN at about 1.3–2.5× flock speed in level chases. About a third of strikes should be stoops that arrive already at speed; 35–55 m/s is defensible, compressed for visibility if needed. Strike geometry should follow the Rome attack census: **121 of 175 from above, 36 from the side, 18 from below**, with **31% of attacks arriving within 5 s of another** ([Storms 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-018-2609-0)). Peregrines at Rome roosts succeed in **23.1% of 328 sequences**. Success is highest in sequences shorter than 1.5 min with fewer than 3 attacks, and singletons are caught more often than flock members ([Zoratto 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-048X.2010.04974.x)). If catches are modelled, Mills' 0.2 m capture radius and 50 ms falcon delay are the published values. Human "predators" chasing StarDisplay flocks show targeting error rising with flock size with no plateau at 5000 ([Hogan 2017](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160564)). This justifies making the falcon's retargeting noisier as local density rises. The app's current schedule, a first sortie at 14–20 s and then one every 28–52 s, is far denser than nature. "At least one catch every evening" at 23% success implies only about 4–5 sequences per evening (my inference). The dense schedule is acceptable for a demo but should be documented, with a realistic mode spacing sorties minutes apart.

**The starling response should be discrete and copied, not radial and continuous.** The app pushes every bird within 32.5 m radially outward with W_FLEE·fear², and alarm boosts cohesion threefold (lines 386, 434–443). That is effectively a flash expansion on every strike, yet real flash expansion follows only **25% of attacks**, is preceded by an attack 83.3% of the time, and leads to a split in only **21.7%** of cases ([Storms 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-018-2609-0)). Under RobotFalcon pursuit across four species, the commonest response is a **≥ 90° collective turn (49–64%)**, then compacting (20–27%), then splits (10–12%). Starling chases yield about **10.7 escape events per chase**, and real peregrines and the robot do not differ ([Storms 2024](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2023.0737)). StarEscape reproduces turns, splits, cordons, columns, flash expansion and blackening from a single rule set ([Papadopoulou 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4)):

| StarEscape element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Stress | Integrates proximity to the predator and decays at a constant rate |
| Escape onset | Stochastic, with probability rising with stress |
| Manoeuvre | Either a ≈ 180° level turn away from the predator or a dive whose steepness grows with proximity |
| Copying | 7 topological neighbours copy the *type* of manoeuvre but set its *geometry* from their own position |
| Alarm | Alarmed birds update every 20 ms instead of 50 ms; compaction emerges from this alone |
| Refractory | A refractory period prevents immediate re-triggering |

Dilution needs no rule of its own. It emerges from the return to 50 ms when the predator leaves, matching the empirical **15.0 ± 2.4 s** dilution latency ([Storms 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-018-2609-0)). Pigeons under RobotFalcon attack show **no increased attraction** to neighbours ([Sankey 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.05.009)), so the app's `W_COH·(1 + 2·alarm)` "selfish herd" boost should go. A constant-magnitude lateral turn-away inside a 30–60 m threat radius, combined with alignment, is enough to reproduce the empirical rise in escape frequency with proximity ([Papadopoulou 2022a](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009772)). Who initiates also matters: fast-turning peripheral initiators produce splits, and central initiators produce collective turns ([Papadopoulou 2022b](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.211898)). A strike from directly behind the flock centre should sometimes split the flock left and right. The StarEscape source code is on Zenodo ([doi:10.5281/zenodo.19664015](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19664015)), and its supplementary parameter table, which was not read here, is the authoritative source for stress, refractory and attack timings.

**Agitation waves are roll waves, so render them through bank.** A bird that rolls sideways and back once (a "zig") exposes more wing to a side-on observer. Copied by 2–7 neighbours, this makes the single dark band seen in the field. A full zigzag makes a double band, and with birds drawn as spheres no band appears at all ([Hemelrijk 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3)). The parameters are the 0.088 s mean hop delay (σ ≈ 10 ms) and copy probability near 1. Fading with distance from the falcon is reproduced only by reducing the maximum bank angle by **0–4% per copy step**, not by lowering copy probability ([Hemelrijk 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-019-2734-4)). Wave speed rises with copy range and NND, falls with reaction time and does not change with flock size (500–8000). Targets: a band speed of about **13–17 m/s**; **2.88 ± 0.19 pulses** about **0.86 s** apart per **3.5 ± 0.23 s** event, occurring about 13 s from strikes during the shadowing phase; and lower contrast at the far side ([Storms 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-018-2609-0); [Hemelrijk 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-019-2734-4)). Under robotic attack, agitation waves appeared only in the largest flock studied, so gate them on N ≳ 1000 ([Papadopoulou 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4)). Blackening should come from orientation, not density. In rendered StarDisplay flocks, darkness correlates −0.82 with the angle between line of sight and wing normal and essentially zero (0.00 to −0.01) with NND. Sharp turns briefly *brighten* a flock as its wings go edge-on ([Costanzo 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11721-021-00207-4)).

## Roost and field data turn waypoints into an evening

The app's turning driver is a wandering waypoint, switched at least every 16 s, plus screen-framing pushes. The field data offer a physical replacement: the **roost**. Rome display flocks of a few hundred to a few thousand birds fly below 100 m "right over the roost", sharp-bordered, in a random walk. Separate columnar masses of tens of thousands fly above 200 m ([Ballerini 2008a](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667)). StarDisplay needed a roost cylinder of **150 m radius** with horizontal turn-back and vertical preferred-height forces for splits, merges and turns to emerge ([Hildenbrandt 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677)). That is the cleanest substitute for waypoints. It comes with the scale-free-chaos caveat above, so the confinement control run must be repeated with it. The app's 400–3000 birds correspond to one Rome display flock (reconstructed sizes 448–2631). Whole murmurations average **30,082 birds (maximum 750,000)** and last a mean **26 min** in the UK ([Goodenough 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0179277)). Faithful "murmuration" scenes therefore need several app-scale flocks plus a distant impostor mass.

**Camera geometry needs to move back.** Real display flocks are 21–143 m long and 5–19 m thick ([Ballerini 2008a](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667)). STARFLAG filmed from 30 m up at an average 100 m range, with reconstructions possible out to 250 m. A 46° vertical field spans only 31 m at the app's nearest distance of 37 m (my arithmetic), so flocks above about 1000 birds overflow the frame. A 100–250 m viewing distance matches both the science footage and N ≥ 1000.

**The evening has a measured timeline.** At a Scottish roost, the first flock arrives R₁ = **0.19·D − 67.56 min before sunset** (D = day length in minutes, r = 0.82). The printed intercept lost its sign; −67.56 reproduces the October mean of 50.2 min and the December–January mean of 14.5 min. Last birds arrive **11 min before sunset in October** and **13–18 min after sunset in midwinter**, so assembly takes **31–47 min**. Overcast or misty evenings advance roosting by **14–28 min** ([Brodie 1980](https://doi.org/10.1080/00063658009476667)). Display duration is U-shaped across the season, shortest near the solstice, and tracks day length (R² = 0.384) more than temperature (R² = 0.144). Size peaks in early February. With predators present, displays more often end in an en-masse descent to roost ([Goodenough 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0179277)). Arrivals come from narrow flight-line sectors. Radar shows morning departure waves at a log-normal modal interval of 3 min, and airspeeds of about 16.5–20.6 m/s that run faster in winter ([Eastwood 1962](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1962.0042)). That interval is the best available proxy for evening sub-flock arrivals. At the high-predation Rome roost, incoming flocks were larger and more compact. At the low-predation roost, small flocks and singletons were commoner, and flocks compacted when *other* flocks nearby showed anti-predator behaviour ([Carere 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2008.08.034)). A "predation pressure" setting should therefore raise sub-flock size, display duration and the probability of a mass descent. No peer-reviewed measurement exists of descent rate, funnel geometry, display-area radius or lux threshold for roost entry. These remain tunable guesses rather than targets.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the definition of an accurate murmuration from "high order and long correlations" to "the right *dynamics* behind those statics". The app already matches the static signatures that made starlings famous: ξ/L ≈ 0.35, ξ_sp/L ≈ 0.36, and Φ within the 0.2 s field range. These turn out to be the least discriminating observables. Goldstone physics, StarDisplay, confined Vicsek swarms and even rigid-rotation droplets can all produce them. The observables that separate a starling model from a generic boid are temporal and geometric: a linear turn front at about 15 m/s that starts at the flock's tips; equal-radius paths that swap front and side; overdamped small fluctuations coexisting with undamped large ones; speed held per bird by a marginal well; a denser rim on a five-bird-thick sheet; and a falcon whose strikes trigger discrete, copied, fading roll waves rather than a radial blast. The app currently scripts or bypasses most of these. Its flock-level holds, centring spring and 4 s noise are workarounds that make the statics come out right, and several of them (the scripted ripple, W_GLOBAL, the OU time constant) could be producing the very signatures being measured.

The practical implication is that the app can serve as a testbed for the open questions, not just an illustration of them. Run with W_GLOBAL = 0, with white noise plus spin inertia, and with per-bird marginal speed, it would show directly whether starling-like scale-freeness needs confinement, coloured noise or neither. No published paper has done that comparison across model families. Three empirical gaps limit how far the app can be pinned down: no measured single-bird heading autocorrelation for starlings, no physical-unit fit of J, J₄, χ or η, and no quantitative data on roost descent or on real flocks' 3-D structure under attack. Until those exist, the app's targets for those quantities should be labelled as inferences.
