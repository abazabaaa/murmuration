# Empirical 3-D structure and collective kinematics of starling flocks: quantitative targets for murmuration.html

Compiled 2026-10-05. Scope: flock geometry, internal structure, speed, turning, positional diffusion and large-scale murmuration morphology, for *Sturnus vulgaris* first (other species labelled "COMPARISON"). This deliberately goes beyond `research/criticality-canonical-references.md` (which already holds Ballerini 2008 aspect ratios, densities, r1 and the correlation/criticality literature); numbers already in that file are repeated only where needed as anchors and are marked "(anchor)".

Conventions. Sim units: 1 u = 0.5 m, cruise 11.5 m/s = 23 u/s, separation radius RS = 2.5 m = 5 u, K = 7. I1 < I2 < I3 are the flock's thickness, middle and longest dimensions; **I1, I3** (bold in sources) are the corresponding unit axes; **V** flock velocity unit vector; **G** gravity. r1 = mean nearest-neighbour distance (NND); r_h = hard-core radius. "Sim:" at the end of each finding is the measurable target or model change for the simulation. Text sources were read in full text (arXiv HTML via the Firecrawl paper index, PMC full text via PubMed) unless marked "abstract only" or "secondary".

## 1. Flock shape: aspect ratios, thickness, orientation to gravity and motion, change in turns and with N

### Takeaway
Display flocks over the Rome roost are thin horizontal sheets with proportions I1:I2:I3 ≈ 1 : 2.8 : 5.6, independent of N. Thickness is 5–19 m and scales as volume^(1/3). The long axis has no fixed relation to the velocity in a straight-flight snapshot. Just before a turn the long axis is usually roughly perpendicular to V, and after the turn it lies along V, because birds turn on equal-radius paths while the flock's absolute orientation stays fixed. During turns the flock plane banks toward the turn and volume compresses, but thickness changes little (model evidence).

### Cited Findings
- **Per-flock table (10 events, N = 448–2631, Rome, 10 fps stereo).** Thickness I1 = 5.29–19.00 m; I2/I1 = 1.90–3.58; I3/I1 = 4.02–8.36. Volumes are 930–33,487 m³. Concavity, the relative volume difference between the convex hull and the α-shape, is 0.03–0.37 and has no typical value. Means are I2/I1 = 2.8 ± 0.4 and I3/I1 = 5.6 ± 1.0 (95% CI). Neither ratio correlates with volume (P = 0.51, P = 0.54) or speed. I1 vs V^(1/3) gives Pearson R² = 0.97, n = 10, P = 1.6×10⁻⁷ (anchor) — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav, arXiv:0802.1667](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667), doi:[10.1016/j.anbehav.2008.02.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2008.02.004). Sim: report I1, I2/I1 and I3/I1 from a PCA or minimum bounding box every second. Target 2.8 ± 0.4 and 5.6 ± 1.0 at every N, with I1 growing as N^(1/3) at fixed density (I1 ≈ 5–8 m = 10–16 u for N ≈ 500–1300 at r1 ≈ 0.7–1.1 m).
- **Orientation to gravity and velocity.** |I1·G| = 0.93 ± 0.04 (per flock 0.80–0.99), |V·G| = 0.13 ± 0.05 (0.06–0.27) and |V·I1| = 0.19 ± 0.08 (0.06–0.41). |V·I3| varies from flock to flock and over time, with no correlation between the long axis and the velocity — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). Sim: target time-averaged |I1·G| ≥ 0.9 and |V·G| ≈ 0.1–0.2. The distribution of the angle between I3 and V over a long run should be broad, not peaked at 0° as in fish-like or Reynolds models.
- **Shape before and after spontaneous turns** (12 turning events, N = 50–595, 170 fps, durations 1.8–12.9 s). At turn start, |I3·V1| is mostly < 0.5 (median 0.32 across the 12 events; long axis 60–90° from the velocity). At turn end, |I3·V2| has a median of 0.895, with 10 of 12 events > 0.5. Exceptions (E11, E12) came just after two flocks merged. At turn start |I1·G| has a median of 0.925 (range 0.35–0.98). |V1·G| has a median of 0.30 (range 0.01–0.94), so turning flocks are not always level — [Attanasi et al. 2015 J R Soc Interface, arXiv:1410.3330](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330), doi:[10.1098/rsif.2015.0319](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2015.0319) (medians computed here from their Table S1). Sim: log |I3·V| just before and just after each turn of more than 60°. Target: before about 0.3 (median), after about 0.9.
- **Banking of the whole flock in a turn.** Event 32-06 (N = 781, about 4 s) kept I3/I2 ≈ 4/3 through the turn. The angle between V and I3 went from slightly over 90° to 0°, while the absolute orientation of the flock barely changed. At turn onset I1 tilted away from G toward the direction of the turn ("the flock's plane does not slide parallel to the ground but undergoes a finite drag", like a banking aircraft). The same velocity-rotates-relative-to-axes behaviour was seen in three other turning flocks (17-06, 25-08, 28-10) — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). Sim: during a turn, the angle between I1 and G should grow by a visible amount, the plane tilting into the turn, and then return to near 0. Check the sign, which should tilt toward the centre of curvature.
- **N-dependence of shape (MODEL, StarDisplay).** Flocks of N = 200 are more spherical and their aspect ratio, volume and altitude vary less than for N = 2000. In the model-vs-data comparison of the 10 Rome flocks, I3/I1 did not depend on N, but I2/I1 rose with N (Kendall τ = 0.51, P = 0.05). Turning compresses the flock and distorts I3/I1 strongly while "thickness remains relatively undisturbed". Without banking the flock becomes oblong and hugs the roost boundary — [Hildenbrandt, Carere & Hemelrijk 2010 Behav Ecol, arXiv:0908.2677](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677), doi:[10.1093/beheco/arq149](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arq149); [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2011 PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0022479) (PMC3150374). Sim: run N = 200 and N = 2000 and check that the larger flock shows larger shape and volume fluctuations, since the empirical I-ratios are N-independent only on average. Also check that turning compresses volume by tens of percent while I1 stays roughly constant.
- **Why shape orientation is random (MODEL).** With low speed variability (StarDisplay speed CV ≈ 0.01–0.015), turns put individuals on equal-length, equal-curvature paths, so the shape reorients relative to V and birds swap positions. In the same group's fish model, raising speed CV from 0.05 to 0.20 raised elongation along V (L/W) from about 1.1 to about 3.5, and the shape stayed oblong through turns as inner birds slowed and outer birds sped up. The authors' testable hypothesis is that birds have lower speed variability than fish — [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2011 PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0022479). Sim: the sim's speed-control stiffness directly sets whether flocks elongate along V. If the sim's flocks are persistently elongated along V (|V·I3| near 1), speed is too adjustable. Target the broad |V·I3| distribution above.
- **Alternative long-axis ratio.** Flocks are "quite elongated, with an aspect ratio L1/L2 which typically ranges between 6 and 8", with L1 the longest axis — [Cavagna et al. 2022 Nat Commun, arXiv:2101.09748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748), doi:[10.1038/s41467-022-29883-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29883-4). This conflicts with Ballerini's I3/I2 ≈ 5.6/2.8 ≈ 2 unless L2 there means the shortest axis. Sim: use the Ballerini definitions and numbers as the primary target and quote 6–8 only as "longest/shortest", which matches Ballerini's 4.0–8.4 range for I3/I1.
- **Concave and columnar shapes under attack.** Under a RobotFalcon, wild flocks (about 20–2000 birds, the Netherlands, 2019) often went "columnar": elongated vertically, previously described only for dunlin. Columnar flocks gave rise to "cordons", thin lines linking two sub-flocks, and to splits — [Papadopoulou et al. 2026 Commun Biol](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4) (PMC13448496; video classification, no 3-D numbers). Sim: the horizontal-sheet target applies to undisturbed display. Under falcon attack, transient vertical elongation (|I1·G| dropping well below 0.9) is realistic, not a bug.

### Inferences
- With r1 ≈ 1 m, I1 ≈ 5–8 m corresponds to only about 5–8 bird layers in thickness. A sim flock of N = 400–1000 should therefore be visibly a "pancake" a handful of birds thick. A levelling force alone is unlikely to give I1/I3 ≈ 0.18; anisotropic cohesion, or banking aerodynamics with vertical roost attraction as in StarDisplay, is the mechanism the literature uses.
- The "front becomes side" reorientation in turns is an automatic consequence of equal-radius turning (Section 4). A sim that reproduces equal-radius turning should also reproduce the |I3·V| before and after statistics without extra rules.

### Gaps
- No starling study reports how the aspect ratios change *during* a turn as a time series. Ballerini gives only one event (I3/I2 ≈ 4/3 constant), and the StarDisplay statements are model output.
- No empirical banking angle of the flock plane (degrees) is published; only the qualitative tilt of I1 in event 32-06.
- Empirical shape vs N below N ≈ 450 (Ballerini) is unavailable in 3-D. Cavagna 2022 covers N = 10–3000 but reports only L1/L2.

## 2. Internal structure: NND distribution, neighbour anisotropy, border-to-centre density gradient, border fraction, hard core, density vs N

### Takeaway
Flocks are very sparse: the packing fraction is < 0.012, and r1 = 0.68–1.51 m varies more than twofold between flocks independently of N. The NND distribution is Poisson-like but cut off below 2r_h ≈ 0.38 m (one wingspan). Nearest neighbours are depleted along the direction of motion and sit laterally, with the anisotropy vanishing after 6–7 neighbours. The border is denser than the centre, with r1 rising about 20–50% from border to core (centre density about 0.3–0.55 of border density). Topological interaction makes border birds sticky: they stay on the border about 3× longer than diffusion predicts.

### Cited Findings
- **NND per flock (m)**, ordered by r1: 0.68 (N 781), 0.73 (1246), 0.79 (1168), 0.87 (834), 1.00 (617), 1.09 (448), 1.25 (1360), 1.30 (534), 1.31 (2631), 1.51 (1856). Densities are 0.80–0.04 m⁻³. ρ ∝ r1⁻³ (n = 10, R² = 0.8, P = 0.0004). Neither ρ nor r1 depends on N (P = 0.39) or speed (P = 0.16). The densest flocks have r1 ≈ 3.5 body lengths ≈ 1.7 wingspans (BL 0.2 m, WS 0.4 m) (anchor values; per-flock list new) — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). Sim: r1 should be 1.4–3.0 u (0.7–1.5 m) and flat in N from 200 to 3000. Measure it on internal birds only, since border birds inflate r1 and create a spurious decrease with N.
- **Hard core.** A hard-sphere fit to P(r1) gives r_h = 0.19 ± 0.02 m (SE over flocks), e.g. 0.17 m (32-06) and 0.20 m (25-11). It does not correlate with r1 (P = 0.56), so the minimum separation 2r_h ≈ 0.38 m ≈ one wingspan is density-independent. P(r) is Poisson-like but with a drop at small r; log P>(r) vs r³ shows a shift of 2r_h relative to Poisson. Packing fraction φ = (4/3)πρr_h³ < 0.012 in all flocks ("extremely sparse… a gas without any structure" in hard-sphere terms) — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). Sim: P(r1) should be essentially zero below about 0.76 u (0.38 m), with a right-skewed body. The sim's separation zone (RS = 5 u = 2.5 m) is about 6.5× the empirical hard core; it acts as a soft "personal space", not the hard core. Add a near-hard repulsion at 0.4 m (0.8 u) if close passes occur.
- **What separation radius gives empirical NND (MODEL).** In StarDisplay (Gaussian-decaying separation, hard sphere 0.2 m, 6.5 topological neighbours), fitting the 10 Rome flocks needed r_sep = 1.6 m → NND 0.69 m, 2.4 m → 0.89 m, 3.0 m → 1.00–1.10 m, 4.0–4.5 m → 1.26–1.33 m and 5.4 m → 1.54 m. NND and the actual interaction radius both scale linearly with r_sep — [Hildenbrandt et al. 2010, arXiv:0908.2677](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677) (Table 2). StarEscape (avoid only the single nearest neighbour, K = 7 for alignment and attraction) gives NND = 0.86 ± 0.08 m at 9.5 ± 0.32 m/s (18 runs) — [Papadopoulou et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4). Sim: by analogy, RS = 2.5 m should yield r1 ≈ 0.9 m (about 1.8 u) if separation is Gaussian-weighted. If the sim gives r1 well above 1.5 m at RS = 5 u, separation is too strong or cohesion too weak.
- **Angular (bearing) distribution of the nearest neighbour.** Shown for 4 flocks as P(cosθ), with θ the angle between the flock velocity and the NN vector; isotropic = 0.5 flat. All 10 flocks show a significant depletion at cosθ ≈ ±1: P(cosθ ≈ ±0.9) = 0.355–0.437, against P ≈ 0.51–0.58 near cosθ = 0. Some flocks peak at cosθ = 0 (neighbour in the plane perpendicular to V); others have two peaks at intermediate angles — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). The angular density map shows "a significant lack of nearest neighbours along the velocity" in every flock, while the location of the maxima (e.g. in the horizontal plane) is not stable between flocks. The 10th neighbour is isotropic. The anisotropy factor γ(n) = (W(n)·V)² is 0.73 (32-06) and 0.70 (25-11) at n = 1 vs 1/3 isotropic, and decays to isotropy between the 6th and 7th neighbour. n_c^(−1/3) is uncorrelated with r1 (R² = 0.0002, P = 0.97), while the metric range does correlate (R² = 0.78) — [Ballerini et al. 2008 PNAS, arXiv:0709.1916](https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.1916), doi:[10.1073/pnas.0711437105](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0711437105). Sim: compute the histogram of cosθ and γ(n) for n = 1…15 on internal birds. Targets: P(|cosθ| > 0.8) ≈ 0.36–0.44, P(cosθ ≈ 0) ≈ 0.52–0.58, γ(1) ≈ 0.7, γ(n) reaching 1/3 at n ≈ 6–7. If the sim is isotropic, make separation stronger front-back than lateral, or give alignment and cohesion a rear blind angle (StarDisplay: 2 × 45° rear blind angle for cohesion and alignment, all-round separation, which reproduced the empirical cosθ distribution for flocks 32-06, 17-06, 25-10 and 25-11: Kendall τ = 0.72, P < 0.01).
- **Neighbour rank-distance law.** The mean distance to the n-th neighbour is linear in n^(1/3), with slope ∝ r1 — [Ballerini et al. 2008 PNAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.1916). Sim: a cheap check of homogeneity. A plot of r_n vs n^(1/3) for n = 1–30 should be a straight line through the origin region.
- **Border-to-centre gradient (r1 vs depth d from the α-shape border, m).** For 25-08, r1 = 1.00 at d = 0.17 m and 1.49 at d = 5.48 m. For 31-01, 1.03 → 1.58 (d = 0.17 → 5.2 m). For 32-06, 0.64 → 0.78 (d = 0.17 → 2.67 m). For 25-10, 0.79 → 0.96 (d = 0.17 → 3.23 m). A Poisson set with the same border stays flat at about 1.26 m. Peeling shells inward (25-08) drops the density of the remaining set from 0.114 to about 0.03–0.065 m⁻³ while the random control stays at 0.11. The gradient is qualitatively present in all 10 flocks — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). Sim: target r1(core)/r1(border) ≈ 1.2–1.5, i.e. core density about 0.3–0.55 of border density. Measure r1 in depth bins of 0.5 m (1 u) from an α-shape or convex-hull border. Most cohesion-centred models give the *opposite* sign (StarDisplay: NND 1.28 m at the border vs 1.24 m in the centre, P < 0.05, *failing* this test).
- **Mechanism for a dense border (MODEL).** A metric-free model needs an *inward* motional bias for edge birds and an *outward* bias for interior birds to fit the Rome density profile — [Pearce & Turner, "Density distributions and depth in flocks", arXiv:1902.08181](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08181) (abstract only; journal version not verified). Topological interactions alone produce longer border residence and sharp ("hard") borders, while metric interactions do not — [Reynolds 2025 J R Soc Interface 22:20250020](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2025.0020) (PMID 40763797; abstract only; supporting data are jackdaw telemetry). Sim: add a depth-dependent bias, inward for birds with an empty half-space of neighbours and slightly outward for deep interior birds, and test for the dense-border profile.
- **Front/back density (balance shift).** The relative offset of the centre of mass from the geometric centre along V is −0.1 to +0.5. Four flocks were front-heavy, two back-heavy and the rest uniform, with no general front-back density correlation (unlike fish schools, denser at the front) — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). Sim: the balance shift should hover near 0 with excursions of either sign. A persistent positive shift, with birds bunching at the front, is non-starling.
- **Border identification and effective dimension.** Border birds are found with an α-shape whose α maximises internal density. In flock 69-10 the count of neighbours within radius R follows M = aR^d̂ with d̂ = 2.3 ± 0.08, a = 0.5 (border effects lower the effective dimension below 3) — [Cavagna et al. 2013 Proc R Soc B, arXiv:1206.4434](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434), doi:[10.1098/rspb.2012.2484](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2012.2484). Sim: compute M(R) for R up to about 5 r1 and check d̂ ≈ 2.3 for N ≈ 1000. A larger d̂ means a more spherical flock than real ones.
- **COMPARISON, transit flocks.** Starling transit flocks (Major & Dill 1978) were less compact than dunlin. Dunlin nearest neighbours were most often behind and below, leaving few neighbours immediately in front and below — [Major & Dill 1978 Behav Ecol Sociobiol 4:111](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00354974) (abstract). The mean NND quoted from that paper is 1.45 m for starlings and 0.70 m for dunlin, vs 1.538 m for rock doves — secondary, via [Pomeroy & Heppner 1992 Auk 109:256](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/auk/vol109/iss2/6/) (DOI not resolved here). Sim: r1 ≈ 1.45 m (2.9 u) is a plausible transit-flight target, at the sparse end of the display range.

### Inferences
- **Border fraction.** No paper gives it explicitly. With d̂ ≈ 2.3 and flock thickness of only 5–8 r1, a large share of birds (plausibly 20–40% for N ≈ 1000) lie within one r1 of the α-shape border. Any r1 or density statistic must exclude them (Ballerini's "internal birds"). Treat this as an estimate, not a measured target.
- The empirical ingredients that separate starlings from generic boids are: a hard core at one wingspan, front-back depletion of neighbours, a denser border, and density independent of N. Each is cheap to measure in the sim and gives a pass/fail test.

### Gaps
- Explicit border fraction vs N is not reported in the papers read.
- The full P(r1) histograms for all 10 flocks are not tabulated, only 32-06 and 25-11.
- The digitised γ(n) values beyond n = 3 obtained from the PNAS figure looked inconsistent with the text (they fell below 1/3), so only γ(1) ≈ 0.7 and "isotropic by n ≈ 6–7" are reported.
- The STARFLAG handbook Part II (Cavagna et al. 2008 Anim Behav 76:237, the border and α-shape methods) was not read directly here.

## 3. Speed: individual speed distribution, fluctuation magnitude, relation to flock speed, accelerations

### Takeaway
Flock (centre-of-mass) speeds are 6.9–15.2 m/s in the 2005–06 Rome sample (mean of 10 flocks ≈ 10.6 m/s) and about 12 ± 2 m/s across the 2007–2020 sample of N = 10–3000. Mean speed is independent of N. Individual speeds are tightly confined around v0, and the published single-bird speed distributions are fitted by a marginal (quartic-like) confinement, not a linear spring. Speed varies little within a flock, and low speed variability is what produces equal-radius turns and position swapping.

### Cited Findings
- **Centre-of-mass speeds of the 10 Ballerini flocks (m/s):** 9.6, 11.1, 8.8, 12.0, 11.2, 10.1, 11.9, 9.1, 15.2, 6.9. The mean is 10.6 m/s (computed here). Density is independent of speed (P = 0.16) and so are the aspect ratios — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). Sim: cruise 11.5 m/s sits in the middle of the range. Allowing flock speed to wander between about 9 and 13 m/s (18–26 u/s) over minutes is realistic.
- **Group speed vs N.** "The average cruising speed of starlings within a flock is about 12 m/s, with typical fluctuations of 2 m/s", which is also the typical flock speed. Mean speed shows no dependence on N (Spearman r_S = −0.13, p = 0.21) over N = 10–3000. Data come from three campaigns: 10 fps (2007–2010), 170 fps (2011–12) and 155 fps (2019–20) — [Cavagna et al. 2022 Nat Commun](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29883-4). Sim: flock mean speed should stay within ±2 m/s of v0 for N = 50 and for N = 3000 alike. Small flocks speeding up indicates a too-soft linear speed spring (entropic push ∝ 1/√(Ng)).
- **Single-bird speed distributions (Cavagna 2022 Fig. 4).** Distributions of v_i/v0 at three values of N are fitted well by the marginal model at all N. Linear control "is unable to fit the experimental distribution even at the largest N, and it is a total disaster in the medium and smallest N cases". The probability of near-zero speed vanishes — [Cavagna et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09748). Sim: histogram v_i/v0 for internal birds. The shape should be narrow-peaked with steep shoulders, i.e. quartic confinement V ∝ (v² − v0²)⁴/v0⁶. See the canonical report for the force law.
- **Model speed variability.** In StarDisplay the coefficient of variation of individual speed is only 0.01, rising to 0.015 even with the speed-restoring force removed, which the authors attribute to aerodynamic forces. A fish model reaches 0.05–0.20 — [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2011 PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0022479) (MODEL). Sim: the instantaneous within-flock speed CV should be of order a few percent (≈ 0.3–0.6 m/s at 11.5 m/s). This is an inference from the model plus the marginal-control fit, not a measured starling value.
- **Accelerations in turns.** Turning is detected per bird as a peak in the modulus of radial acceleration. Accelerations at 170 fps are dominated by the 10 Hz wing-beat zig-zag and must be low-pass filtered (2nd-order Butterworth, cutoff about ω_flap/30) to extract the turn. The peak radial acceleration decays only weakly from the first to the last bird to turn — [Attanasi et al. 2014 Nat Phys, arXiv:1303.7097](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097), doi:[10.1038/nphys3035](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3035). Sim: per-bird peak lateral acceleration should be about the same for early and late turners (no attenuation). Any wing-beat jitter added for visuals must not enter the steering dynamics.
- **COMPARISON, jackdaws.** "All flocks reduced their speed during turns, probably because of constraints on individual flight capability". Turn rate increases with turn angle so that turn duration is constant — [Ling et al. 2019 J R Soc Interface 16:20190450](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2019.0450) (PMID 31640502; abstract only, magnitudes not extracted). COMPARISON, rock doves: in the analysed release the flock was "executing a right turn of approximately 90°, while losing altitude, and was accelerating"; flight speed about 25 km/h (context of this figure in the PDF not verified) — [Pomeroy & Heppner 1992](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/auk/vol109/iss2/6/). MODEL (StarDisplay): banking lowers effective lift, so birds lose altitude and gain horizontal speed after the apex, then return to level flight and cruise speed — [Hildenbrandt et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677). Sim: in starling-like turns, keep |v| nearly constant (equal-radius turning requires it). A small altitude loss with a matching speed gain during sharp turns is realistic. A systematic slow-down in turns is jackdaw-like and should be optional.

### Inferences
- The sim's current speed law (linear W_SPD on the group speed plus a cubic W_SPD4 on the individual deviation) is already close to the marginal form. Fitting the *individual* v_i/v0 histogram, and checking that it does not change with N, is the cleanest discriminating test.
- Low speed variability is a *shape* parameter as well as a kinematic one (Section 1). Raising speed adjustability to get more "life" will elongate the flock along V and suppress position swapping in turns.

### Gaps
- No paper read gives the numerical SD of individual speeds within a starling flock (e.g. in m/s or as a CV). Cavagna 2022's "fluctuations of 2 m/s" refers to the flock-to-flock distribution of group speed; its Fig. 4 shows the single-bird distribution but not its width in text.
- No starling-specific numbers were found for radial acceleration magnitude (m/s²), turn radius or turn angular velocity in the papers read (Attanasi 2014 shows acceleration curves only graphically).
- The speed change during starling turns has not been measured directly. Equal-radius turning implies near-constant speed, but this is an inference stated by Attanasi 2014 (Appendix A), not a measured percentage.

## 4. Turning: equal-radius vs parallel turning, radii, angular velocities, banking, speed in turns, reshuffling of positions

### Takeaway
Starlings turn on **equal-radius paths**: each bird has about the same curvature radius around a different centre, and trajectories cross. They do not follow parallel paths with a common centre as a rigid body would. The flock's absolute orientation stays nearly fixed while its velocity rotates relative to the body axes, so lateral birds end at the front or back and vice versa (rock doves: 76% of birds change quadrant). Spontaneous turns start at the lateral tips of the long axis among birds that deviate *persistently*, not strongly. They propagate without attenuation at 9.4–21.3 m/s with a/c_s ≈ 25–100 ms, and individual trajectories are nearly planar.

### Cited Findings
- **Equal-radius evidence (12 events).** Individual trajectories show "very similar radii of curvature and trajectory crossing". Network test: θ_r, the mean rotation of all pair vectors r_ij measured in the absolute frame, stays small while θ_V (the rotation of the flock velocity) grows strongly. For parallel-path rigid turning θ_r = θ_V. This held in all 12 events. Example turn: 120° (event E1, N = 176) — [Attanasi et al. 2015 J R Soc Interface](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330). Sim: compute θ_r(t) = ⟨arccos(r̂_ij(0)·r̂_ij(t))⟩ and θ_V(t) over a turn. Target: θ_r/θ_V ≪ 1 at turn end (equal-radius). A rigidly rotating flock fails.
- **Speed is the reason.** In an equal-radius turn each bird keeps |v| constant with φ̇ = v/R. A parallel turn would require outer birds to fly faster, "which is clearly biologically unreasonable". The propagating quantity is curvature κ = 1/R (the ISM "spin") — [Attanasi et al. 2014 Nat Phys](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097), Appendix A. Ballerini 2008 Anim Behav makes the same energetic argument. Sim: per-bird turn radius R_i = v_i²/a_⊥,i should be nearly equal across the flock during a turn (target CV of R_i at peak turning ≲ 0.2; this threshold is an inference). Turn centres should be spread over a region as large as the flock.
- **Position reshuffling.** Before the turn the flock flies with its long axis roughly perpendicular to V; birds on the sparse lateral tips (which started the turn) end at the front or back, and front/back birds end on the sides — [Attanasi et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330). COMPARISON, rock doves (flock of 21, cohesive cluster of 12–16): 75.6% ± 12.5 SD of birds (n = 7 releases) were in a different flock quadrant at the end of a turn than at the start. Some subgroups kept their mutual positions; crossing paths came from arcs "of different radii, or of similar radius but which originated at different centers" — [Pomeroy & Heppner 1992 Auk](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=22345&context=auk). Sim: label birds by quadrant (front-left, front-right, back-left, back-right relative to V) at turn start. Target: more than about 60–75% in a different quadrant after a ≥ 90° turn.
- **Where turns start.** The ten first-ranked birds lie along the longest axis I3: |I3·d0/|d0|| = 0.56–1.00, median 0.91. They sit near the tip, with |I3·d0|/d_max mostly > 0.5. They are on the sides, not at the front: |V1·d0/|d0|| is low in most events. The top-5 initiators are close together (their mutual distance does not scale with L) — [Attanasi et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330); [Attanasi et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097). Sim: if turns are triggered spontaneously, seed them from birds at the extremes of the I3 axis. Then check that the measured initiator position (mean of the first 10 by peak lateral acceleration) has |I3·d̂0| ≈ 0.9.
- **What triggers turns.** The dealignment time factor δ_i (fraction of time a bird's C_i = v̂_i·V̂ is below the median) grows with distance along I3. Initiators are among the highest-δ birds but not the highest-amplitude ones: "what really is distinctive… is how persistently they deviate". Most edge-bird deviations point outward. Initiators then move *inward* in most events (V2·d0 < 0 in 9 of 12), "pushing" the flock to turn away from the initiating edge — [Attanasi et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330). Sim: to make spontaneous turns, give tip birds persistent (seconds-long, OU-like) heading noise rather than larger white noise. Expect the flock to turn *away* from the initiating tip.
- **Propagation.** Turn propagation speed c_s per event: 10.09, 21.32, 16.19, 11.37, 11.93, 18.85, 19.23, 18.66, 17.70, 13.77, 9.41 and 10.98 m/s (N = 50–595). Polarisation at 170 fps is 0.757–0.959. The ranking curve r(t) ∝ t^3.2 on average, so x ∝ t^1.07 (linear). a/c_s = 25–100 ms, mean about 50 ms. The turn plane holds: V·n3 fluctuates around 0 during the turn (anchor c_s range; per-event list new) — [Attanasi et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7097). Sim: c_s target 9–21 m/s = 19–43 u/s. Each bird's trajectory during a turn should lie in a plane (|v̂·n3| ≈ 0).
- **Flock-level banking (see Section 1).** The flock plane tilts into the turn (I1 moves away from G and toward V) — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667). MODEL: individual banking angle correlates with path curvature. The turn starts with banking, which costs effective lift, so altitude drops and vertical speed rises; banking reduces after the apex. In small model flocks, flock banking correlates with mean individual banking. StarDisplay's roll gains (w_βin = 10, w_βout = 1) were tuned to Rome videos so that birds roll in faster than they roll out — [Hildenbrandt et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677); [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2015 PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0126913). Sim: render bank angle φ_b = atan(a_⊥/g) per bird (for a coordinated turn). Make roll-in faster than roll-out, and let the flock plane tilt toward the turn centre.
- **Turning initiates during predator escape.** Under robotic-falcon attack, collective turns last about 1 s. They co-occur most often with splits, columnar flocking or compacting. Splits occur when part of the flock turns and the rest does not follow, or when two early responders turn in opposite directions (typically when the predator approaches the middle of the flock from behind) — [Papadopoulou et al. 2026 Commun Biol](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4). Sim: a falcon approach from directly behind the flock centre should sometimes produce a two-way split. A 90° escape turn should take about 1 s per bird.
- **COMPARISON, jackdaws.** Turn duration is constant across turn angles (turn rate ∝ angle); speed drops in turns; initiators are anywhere in transit flocks but at the front in mobbing flocks; information-transfer speed rises with N then saturates — [Ling et al. 2019 J R Soc Interface](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2019.0450) (abstract only).

### Inferences
- A useful single sim test: the angle between I3 and V before and after the turn, θ_r/θ_V, and the quadrant-change fraction should all come out right at once if birds turn on equal-radius paths at near-constant speed. If the sim's alignment acts directly on heading (first-order), turns will be diffusive and attenuated. The canonical report recommends inertial spin (ISM) for linear propagation.
- With v ≈ 10–12 m/s and a typical display turn completing about 90–180° in a few seconds, equal-radius turns imply radii of order 10–30 m and lateral accelerations of a few m/s² (R = v·t/θ; e.g. 11 m/s through 90° in 2 s gives R ≈ 14 m and a ≈ 8.6 m/s²). This is an order-of-magnitude inference, not a published measurement.

### Gaps
- No published starling values were found for individual turning radius distributions, angular velocities (°/s), peak radial accelerations (m/s²) or bank angles. Attanasi 2014 shows radial acceleration curves only graphically; Hildenbrandt 2010 tuned roll rates qualitatively to videos.
- Pomeroy & Heppner's turning radii and banking were not quantified in the extracted text.

## 5. Positional diffusion of birds within the flock (Cavagna et al. 2013 Proc R Soc B)

### Takeaway
In the centre-of-mass frame, starlings are strongly super-diffusive (α = 1.73 ± 0.07, n = 6 flocks) and anisotropic. Motion is largest along the wing axis (perpendicular to both V and G, α1 = 1.78), smaller along V (α2 = 1.63) and smallest along gravity (α3 = 1.44). Neighbours reshuffle purely by diffusion, but slowly: half of the 10 nearest neighbours change in about 3.5 s. Border birds stay on the border about 3× longer than diffusion predicts (τ ≈ 2.5 s vs 0.8 s).

### Cited Findings
- **Centre-of-mass diffusion.** δr²(t) = D t^α, fitted over lags of 0.4–1.5 s. Mean α = 1.73 ± 0.07 and D = 0.036 ± 0.004 over six flocks. Per flock: 28-10 (N 1246, 1.5 s, 785 full trajectories) α 1.83 ± 0.01; 48-17 (871) 1.73; 49-05 (797) 1.71; 58-06 (442) 1.69; 69-09 (239) 1.64; 69-10 (1129, 3.4 s) 1.77. D(×10⁻²) = 3.5–4.1. Synthetic Brownian controls give a 95th-percentile α* ≤ 1.64. Over the late window 1–3.4 s in 69-10, α = 1.66 — [Cavagna et al. 2013 Proc R Soc B](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434), doi:[10.1098/rspb.2012.2484](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2012.2484). Sim: compute MSD in the CoM frame for internal birds over lags of 0.4–1.5 s. Target α ≈ 1.7 (accept 1.6–1.85).
- **Anisotropy (principal axes of the displacement tensor).** α1 = 1.78 ± 0.08, D1 = 0.021 ± 0.003; α2 = 1.63 ± 0.04, D2 = 0.008 ± 0.004; α3 = 1.44 ± 0.12, D3 = 0.004 ± 0.004. Orientation: |u1·V| = 0.11 ± 0.04 and |u1·G| = 0.27 ± 0.07, so the maximum-diffusion axis is about the wing axis. |u2·V| = 0.90 ± 0.03. |u3·G| = 0.83 ± 0.11, so the minimum-diffusion axis is about gravity. The rationale: heading errors δθ displace a bird ∝ δθ laterally but ∝ δθ² along V, and gravity further suppresses vertical motion — [Cavagna et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434). Sim: diagonalise the MSD tensor. Targets: max axis ⟂ V and G; ordering D_lateral : D_along-V : D_vertical ≈ 5 : 2 : 1 in coefficient, and α_lat > α_V > α_vert.
- **Mutual (neighbour) diffusion is suppressed.** α_m = 1.58 ± 0.2, D_m = 0.011 ± 0.005, about 3× smaller than D. Per flock D_m(×10⁻²) is 0.37–1.7. Correlated velocity fluctuations make neighbours drift together — [Cavagna et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434). Sim: the ratio D_m/D ≈ 0.1–0.45 is a direct probe of how correlated neighbour fluctuations are. Too large a ratio means neighbours wander independently (noise too local).
- **Neighbour reshuffling.** The overlap Q_M(t) of the M nearest neighbours decays smoothly. "It takes about 3.5 seconds to change only half of 10 neighbours" (flock 69-10). The decay is fitted by Q_M(t) = (1 + c t^(α_m/2)/M^(1/d̂))^(−d̂), with c = 0.048 fitted, d̂ = 2.3 and α = 1.7, i.e. purely diffusive with no neighbour-keeping mechanism — [Cavagna et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434). StarEscape, tuned to these data, gives Q(3 s) = 0.58 ± 0.06 — [Papadopoulou et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4). Sim: track Q_10(t). Target Q_10(3.5 s) ≈ 0.5 and Q(3 s) ≈ 0.55–0.6, with the M-dependence following the formula above.
- **Border persistence.** The border survival probability P(t) has a fast initial drop and a long tail. An exponential fit for 69-10 gives τ = 2.5 s, while the diffusion estimate τ_diff = (l_B²/D)^(1/α) = 0.8 s, about 3× shorter. The internal-bird survival decays much faster and matches super-diffusion (fitted k1 = 1.42 ± 0.15, consistent across flocks). Proposed reasons: border birds have no neighbours outward, do not stray far, and are pushed back by interior neighbours (linked to the dense border) — [Cavagna et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4434). Reynolds 2025 argues that topological interaction alone produces both the long border residence and sharp borders — [doi:10.1098/rsif.2025.0020](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2025.0020) (abstract only). Border vs interior CoM diffusion in 28-10: α = 1.78 ± 0.03 (border) vs 1.86 ± 0.02 (interior), D = 4.2 vs 3.1 (×10⁻²). Sim: mark border birds each frame and measure the mean residence time on the border. Target ≈ 2.5 s, about 3× the time for internal birds to move one r1.
- **Model reproductions (MODEL).** StarDisplay gives α = 1.68–1.76 for the same four flocks (empirical about 1.70). The diffusion *coefficient* matches only if each bird avoids just its single nearest neighbour (D = 1.49–2.85, compared with 0.22–0.29 when avoiding 6–7, in the authors' plotting units). Avoiding one neighbour also raises flock volume toward the empirical one (28-10: 1840 m³) and lowers polarisation — [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2015 PLoS ONE 10:e0126913](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0126913) (PMC4436282). Sim: if the sim's internal diffusion is too slow or the neighbour overlap too sticky, make separation act on the single nearest neighbour (as in StarDisplay-2015 and StarEscape) rather than summing over K = 7.

### Inferences
- **Units of D (inference, flagged).** The paper does not state units. Treating t in frames (0.1 s) and δr² in m² makes the border-time formula consistent: for 69-10, τ_diff = 0.8 s requires l_B ≈ 1.2 m ≈ r1, whereas t in seconds would imply l_B ≈ 0.16 m. Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt (2015) describe the empirical time axis as labelled "(x 10s)", which is ambiguous but consistent with 0.1 s units. Converting, D ≈ 0.036 × 10^1.73 ≈ 1.9 m² s^(−1.73). That gives an rms CoM-frame displacement of about 1.4 m after 1 s and about 2 m after 1.5 s. Per axis at 1 s it is about 1.1 m lateral, 0.6 m along V and 0.3 m vertical (variance ratio about 11 : 3 : 1). In sim units: rms ≈ 2.8 u after 1 s and 4 u after 1.5 s.
- **Time to traverse the flock (extrapolation, flagged).** Extrapolating the lateral law beyond the 1.5 s fit window, a bird needs about 15–20 s to diffuse 15 m (half-width of a 30 m flock). Real MSD must saturate at the flock size; StarDisplay MSD curves bend over once birds have explored the flock. No empirical full-traversal time exists.
- Super-diffusion with α ≈ 1.7 means displacements are correlated over seconds: velocity fluctuations relative to the CoM decorrelate as t^(−ξ) with ξ = 2 − α ≈ 0.3. Coloured (OU-like) noise with τ of seconds, as the sim's NOISE_TAU = 4 s, naturally produces 1 < α < 2 on 0.4–1.5 s lags. This is a plausible but untested match.

### Gaps
- The trajectory windows are short (1.5–4.6 s at 10 fps), so the long-time regime, the flock-traversal time and the saturation of MSD are unmeasured. The 2019–20 155 fps data and the 2025 panning rig (acquisitions up to 12.5 s) could provide this, but no diffusion analysis of them was found.
- No independent confirmation of D's units from the journal version was obtained.

## 6. Large-scale murmuration morphology (thousands to hundreds of thousands of birds)

### Takeaway
Quantitative 3-D data exist only for single flocks of ≤ 3000 birds (Rome). Whole murmurations of 10⁴–10⁵ birds are characterised only by citizen-science counts and durations, 2-D opacity, and categorical video classifications of escape patterns (wave, blackening, split, cordon, columnar, vacuole, flash expansion, dilution, compacting). No peer-reviewed computer-vision study was found that measures 3-D shape, curvature of density bands, or merge/split rates of whole murmurations (Rome, UK or Danish "sort sol").

### Cited Findings
- **Size and duration (UK citizen science).** n = 1,066 confirmed murmurations. Mean 30,082 ± 6,699 (SEM) birds, maximum 750,000; mean duration 26 min; size peaks in early February. Predators were present at 29.6% of events and explain about 40% of the variance in size (anchor) — [Goodenough et al. 2017 PLoS ONE 12:e0179277](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0179277). Sim: a "murmuration" scene should host flocks of 10³–10⁵ birds. At the sim's N, it represents one sub-flock of a larger display.
- **Display flocks vs whole murmurations.** The analysed Rome flocks were "smaller in size, ranging from a few hundred to several thousand birds… very compact, with sharp-bordered edges", performing a random walk above the roost in quasi-steady state. Larger aggregations up to tens of thousands are also seen — [Ballerini et al. 2008 Anim Behav](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.1667); [Ballerini et al. 2008 PNAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.1916). Sim: the 3-D targets of Sections 1–5 apply to sharp-bordered sub-flocks of a few hundred to a few thousand birds, not to an entire 10⁵-bird murmuration.
- **Sub-flock splitting and merging (MODEL plus video).** StarDisplay reproduces flock splitting and merging seen in Rome videos (Termini roost). The roost is modelled as a cylinder of radius 150 m, with horizontal and vertical roost attraction. Larger model flocks develop larger, more diverse sub-flocks (ξ/L slope 0.44 with 6.5 neighbours and 0.79 with 50, vs 0.35 empirical), and their shape and volume vary more; part of the flock turns back toward the roost earlier and loses height, deforming the whole — [Hildenbrandt et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2677); [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0022479). In Attanasi 2015, two consecutive turns right after two flocks merged (E11, E12) showed atypical geometry, with I3 more aligned to gravity than I1 — [Attanasi et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.3330). Sim: a roost radius of about 150 m (300 u) with N ≥ 2000 should give spontaneous sub-flock formation. Just after merges, expect temporarily non-flat shapes.
- **Collective-escape morphology (Rome, real falcons).** 182 hunting sequences, 67 analysed frame by frame: 795 collective events, 210 attacks. Blackening was most common (n = 289) and vacuoles rarest (n = 5). Wave events last 3.5 ± 0.23 s with 2.88 ± 0.19 pulses about 0.86 s apart; 78.3% of flash expansions did not lead to a split; flock dilution takes 15.0 ± 2.4 s after an attack (anchor) — [Storms et al. 2019 Behav Ecol Sociobiol 73:10](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-018-2609-0). Waves move away from the predator at 3.7–25 m/s, faster than the flock (≈ 10.6 m/s) (anchor) — [Procaccini et al. 2011 Anim Behav](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2011.07.006). Sim: see the canonical report for the wave and escape targets.
- **Robotic-falcon escapes (the Netherlands, 2019).** 19 flocks of about 20–2000 birds were pursued for 20 s to 2 min (mean about 1 min); 10 showed co-occurring patterns. Agitation waves appeared only in the largest flock. Individual manoeuvres were level turns, dives and diving turns. New patterns were "columnar flocking" (vertical elongation) and "cordons" (thin bridges between two sub-flocks, formed when the top of a column turns and the bottom follows with a delay). Model hysteresis: compacting during a turn is followed by expansion, and a single parameter set yields either a compact spherical flock or a wide diluted one depending on prior state — [Papadopoulou et al. 2026 Commun Biol](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4). Sim: under attack, let lower-flock birds dive (pitch toward the ground, more steeply the closer the falcon). This should produce columns, cordons and splits. Density after an attack should overshoot (compact, then dilute) rather than relax monotonically.
- **2-D shape catalogue (COMPARISON, rosy starling *Pastor roseus*, India).** 12 "canonical shapes" of aerial flocks moving to roost, with shape dynamics and transitions driven by predator presence — [Divin & Jhawar 2026 Integr Comp Biol 66](https://doi.org/10.1093/icb/icag027) (PMID 42024435; abstract only, shape names and rates not extracted). Sim: a candidate for a categorical shape-frequency target once the shape list is obtained.
- **Opacity** (anchor): projected opacity Θ′ ≈ 0.25–0.6 across very different flock sizes — [Pearce et al. 2014 PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1402202111). See the canonical report.

### Inferences
- For a browser sim, the measurable large-scale targets are mostly categorical or temporal. Examples: sub-flocks in the roughly 300–3000 bird range that are sharp-bordered and flat; splits that coincide with turns; merges followed by a short period of non-flat shape; wave pulses at 0.7–2 Hz under attack; dilution over about 15 s after an attack. Curvature of density bands in 2-D projections arises mainly from orientation and projection (thin sheets seen edge-on look dark), which is why the canonical report's opacity meter is the best quantitative proxy.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed quantitative (computer-vision) study of whole-murmuration geometry was found: band curvature, sub-flock size distribution, merge or split rates, or 3-D extent for UK, Rome or Danish "sort sol" displays, 2018–2026. Web search returned only popular articles. Hemelrijk's 2021 *Swarm Intelligence* paper on causes of flock darkness (doi:10.1007/s11721-021-00207-4) and Carere et al. 2009 *Anim Behav* 77:101 (aerial flocking patterns under predation risk, doi:10.1016/j.anbehav.2008.08.034) are likely the closest sources but were not read (paywalled or not indexed).
- Shape categories and transition rates from Divin & Jhawar 2026 were not extracted (abstract only).
