# Individual starling flight dynamics, manoeuvrability and sensorimotor limits (inputs to the agent model)

Scope: single-bird dynamics and perception that bound or calibrate the murmuration agents. Morphology is excluded because the repo already covers it. Starling (*Sturnus vulgaris*, about 75–90 g) data comes first. Data from other species is labelled **PROXY** and gives that species' mass. Every reference was checked by DOI, PMID or full-text retrieval during this session unless it is marked **UNVERIFIED**. Each finding ends with "→ SIM:", a one-line implication for the model.

Current model, read from `murmuration.html`: CRUISE = 23 u/s (11.5 m/s), VMIN = 12 u/s (6 m/s), VMAX = 30 u/s (15 m/s), MAX_ACC = 16 u/s² (8 m/s²), 1 u = 0.5 m. The noise is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with τ = 4 s. The wing-beat frequency is drawn per bird from 7–11 Hz. Bank = atan(a_lat/9), clamped to ±1.1 rad (±63°), and relaxes with a rate of 6 s⁻¹. Speed control includes a cubic term.

## 1. Wing-beat frequency and amplitude; intermittent (flap-glide / flap-bound) flight

### Takeaway
Starlings never flap continuously in steady flight. In a wind tunnel at 8–18 m/s they alternate flapping with non-flapping phases. Glides dominate at low speed and bounds become more common as speed rises. Free-flying migrating starlings beat their wings at about 10 Hz. Wing-beat frequency rises with speed. The share of time spent flapping is highest at the slowest speed (8 m/s).

### Cited Findings
- Starlings in a variable-speed wind tunnel flew at 8–18 m/s. Flight at every speed alternated flapping and non-flapping phases, and the non-flapping postures were glides, partial bounds and bounds. Glides were the most frequent posture and lasted longer than bounds, but the share of bounds "increased markedly with increasing flight speed". This is a shift from flap-gliding at slow speeds to flap-bounding at fast speeds. → SIM: add a two-state flap/non-flap animation with a speed-dependent posture: wings extended (glide) below about 12 m/s and wings folded (bound) above it. — [Tobalske 1995, J Exp Biol 198:1259, PMID 9319121](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.198.6.1259)
- In the same study, wing-beat frequency and EMG intensity rose with flight speed. Flapping and non-flapping cycle duration, the percentage of each cycle spent flapping, and the number of wing beats per cycle were all greatest at 8 m/s. Wing-beat amplitude was smallest at intermediate speeds, but the differences were not significant. Birds carrying EMG implants paused less often and for shorter times than non-implanted birds. → SIM: make the flap duty cycle fall with speed, and make frequency rise modestly with speed rather than being a fixed per-bird constant. — [Tobalske 1995](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.198.6.1259) (abstract only; the per-speed tables could not be retrieved, see Gaps)
- Six migrating starlings, observed optically at Falsterbo, beat their wings at **10.2 ± 0.86 Hz (n = 6)** with airspeed 17.3 ± 1.75 m/s. The flight style was recorded as "bounding and continuous flapping". The paper's benchmark body uses mass 88.4 g, span 0.384 m and wing area 0.0251 m². → SIM: centre the per-bird frequency near 10 Hz (for example 9–12 Hz). The current 7–11 Hz range is slightly low. — [Pennycuick 2001, J Exp Biol 204:3283](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.204.19.3283) (retrieved from [Tethys PDF](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/publications/Pennycuick_2001.pdf))
- Review: flap-bounding saves energy at relatively fast speeds, and body and tail lift during bounds is critical to that saving. Flap-gliding is thought to cost less than continuous flapping at most speeds. Some species switch from flap-gliding at slow speeds to flap-bounding at fast speeds. Birds above about 0.3 kg cannot bound. → SIM: a starling at about 80 g is well inside the bounding size range, so drawing bounds is realistic. — [Tobalske 2010, Bioinspir Biomim 5:045004](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-3182/5/4/045004)
- **PROXY, zebra finch (13.2 g, n = 4):** the birds flap-bounded at every speed from 0 to 14 m/s. The percentage of time spent flapping fell as speed increased. During bounds the body produced lift of up to 0.0206 N (15.9 % of body weight) at 10 m/s. → SIM: this qualitatively supports a duty cycle that falls with speed. — [Tobalske, Peacock & Dial 1999, J Exp Biol 202:1725](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.202.13.1725)
- **PROXY, budgerigar (~30–35 g):** the birds flap-glided at low speeds and flap-bounded at high speeds. The percentage of time spent flapping generally fell at intermediate speeds. — [Tobalske & Dial 1994, J Exp Biol 187:1](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.187.1.1)
- **PROXY, magpie (~160–200 g) and pigeon (~400 g), wind tunnel:** both species alternated steady-speed phases with phases of acceleration and deceleration, and often used non-flapping phases while decelerating. They used a vortex-ring gait to accelerate and a continuous-vortex gait or a non-flapping phase to decelerate. In steady flight, wing-beat frequency changed little with speed. → SIM: tie flap bursts to positive longitudinal acceleration and glides or bounds to deceleration. This visual cue would make the animation reflect the dynamics. — [Tobalske & Dial 1996, J Exp Biol 199:263](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.199.2.263)
- **PROXY, in flocks (pigeon, ~400 g):** wing-beat frequency was 6.6–7.0 Hz in cluster flocks, 6.5 ± 0.2 Hz in pairs and 5.5 ± 0.2 Hz solo. Flock birds "flap much faster and engage in banked turns". → SIM: the flock context may raise wing-beat frequency by roughly 20 %. No starling data exists. — [Usherwood et al. 2011, Nature 474:494](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10164) (numbers taken from the search abstract; PubMed record [PMID 21697942](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21697942/))
- **Caution on wind-tunnel frequencies (PROXY, hirundines ~15–20 g):** at comparable flight conditions, free-flying barn swallows and house martins tracked by radar had effective wing-beat frequencies 40 % and 32 % lower than birds in a wind tunnel. → SIM: prefer free-flight values (Pennycuick's 10.2 Hz) over tunnel values when choosing the animation frequency. — [Liechti & Bruderer 2002, J Exp Biol 205:2461](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.205.16.2461)
- Radar and cine data give wing-beat frequency for 153 species. For 41 species with passerine-type flight they also give the durations of flapping and pause phases. The starling entry exists in this paper, but its values were not retrieved here. — [Bruderer et al. 2010, Ibis 152:272](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1474-919X.2010.01014.x)

### Inferences
- A reasonable animation rule is f ≈ 10 Hz ± 1 Hz between birds, rising about 10 % from 8 to 14 m/s, with a flapping duty cycle of roughly 0.6–0.8 at cruise. The duty cycle is my estimate; Tobalske's tables were not retrievable. Each pause should be several wing beats long. In a flock, bias birds toward more flapping during manoeuvres and alarm. The current `1 + 0.5·alarm` frequency boost is plausible in direction.

### Gaps
- Tobalske 1995 gives per-speed starling values for frequency (Hz), amplitude (deg), flap and pause durations (s) and % flapping. Only the abstract could be retrieved (paywall), so no per-speed numbers are given here. My recollection is about 12–14 Hz in the tunnel, but that is **UNVERIFIED** and should not be used without checking the paper.
- No study found measures intermittent-flight statistics for starlings inside murmurations or flocks.
- Bruderer et al. 2010 has starling flap and pause durations from radar, but they were not retrieved.

## 2. Speeds: minimum-power, maximum-range, observed, maximum and dive

### Takeaway
Two kinds of evidence place starling minimum-power speed at about 10–12 m/s: wind-tunnel kinematics, and the ratio of migration speed to Vmp. Starlings in winter flocks over Rome flew at mean speeds of 8–19 m/s, with most flocks between 10 and 13 m/s. Migrating starlings fly faster, at about 17 m/s airspeed. The current 15 m/s cap is below observed flock means.

### Cited Findings
- Wing-beat kinematics and EMG suggested a **minimum power speed near 12 m/s**, with power increasing at both slower and faster speeds. → SIM: 11.5 m/s cruise is consistent with Vmp; keep it. — [Tobalske 1995](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.198.6.1259)
- Metabolic power measured in two starlings rose from 10.4 to 14.9 W as speed went from **6.3 to 14.4 m/s**. This was consistent with the U-shaped curve the aerodynamic models predict. → SIM: 6.3–14.4 m/s is a demonstrated sustainable range, so VMIN = 6 m/s is reasonable. — [Ward et al. 2001, J Exp Biol 204:3311, PMID 11606605](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.204.19.3311)
- Migrating starlings flew at an airspeed of **17.3 ± 1.75 m/s (n = 6)**. A search-result summary of the same paper reports the starling's relative airspeed (observed ÷ calculated Vmp) as 1.70, "more than two standard deviations above Vmp". By my calculation that implies a calculated Vmp of about 10.2 m/s. → SIM: transit flight runs well above Vmp. — [Pennycuick 2001](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.204.19.3283)
- Speeds of starling flocks in Rome measured by 3-D stereo. In 21 flocks of 122–4268 birds, mean speeds were 9.96, 12.06, 12.47, 12.57, 10.07, 11.22, 10.75, 8.13, 9.99, 10.68, 14.02, **19.17** (N = 4268), 14.38, 10.13, 10.81, 10.24, 11.97, 12.04, 14.16, 13.24 and 9.50 m/s. The range is **8.1–19.2 m/s** and the median is about 11.2 m/s. Neighbouring birds' velocities differ by only Q_int ~ 10⁻² (|v_i − v_j|²/2v₀², normalised). → SIM: allow VMAX of at least 18–20 m/s, and let the flock-mean speed vary between episodes from about 8 to 14 m/s rather than being fixed at 11.5. — [Bialek et al. 2014, PNAS 111:7212, PMID 24785504](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1324045111) (table read from [arXiv 1307.5563 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/1307.5563))
- Flock speed during collective turns was **7–12 m/s** (12 turning events, Rome). The theory assumes speed v₀ stays constant during the turn and is the same for all birds. → SIM: in starling turns speed is held roughly constant. — [Attanasi et al. 2014, Nature Physics 10:691, PMID 25264452](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3035)
- Spontaneous collective turns lasted **1.8–12.9 s**. Birds follow equal-radius paths, "ensuring turning at approximately constant speed", and the turns are nearly planar. — [Attanasi et al. 2015, J R Soc Interface 12:20150319, PMID 26236825](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2015.0319)
- Two published starling ABMs used these speeds: StarDisplay cruise speed **10 m/s**, mass 80 g, CL/CD 3.3, default lift 0.78 N, drag = thrust 0.24 N, speed-control time constant 1 s. In the StarEscape model, simulated flocks averaged 9.5 ± 0.32 m/s with nearest-neighbour distance 0.86 ± 0.08 m. → SIM: a speed-relaxation time of about 1 s is the precedent; check the effective time constant of W_SPD. — [Hildenbrandt, Carere & Hemelrijk 2010, Behav Ecol 21:1349](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arq149) (parameter table from [arXiv 0908.2677](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0908.2677)); [Papadopoulou et al. 2026, Commun Biol 9:1055, PMID 42151575](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-10173-4) (preprint [bioRxiv](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.10.27.620514))
- Across 138 species, mean equivalent airspeeds range from about 8 to 23 m/s and scale as mass^0.12–0.13. This is shallower than the geometric-similarity prediction of mass^(1/6). — [Alerstam et al. 2007, PLoS Biol 5:e197, PMID 17645390](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.0050197) (the starling's row is in the supplement and was not retrieved)
- GPS tracking of migrating starlings found a maximum average **ground** speed of 110 km/h (about 31 m/s, wind-assisted). → SIM: not an airspeed limit. — [Vīgants et al. 2023, J Avian Biol e03080](https://doi.org/10.1111/jav.03080) (search snippet only; **partially verified**)
- **PROXY, pigeon (0.41–0.53 kg):** estimated ground speed was 18.6 m/s in flocks and 15.2 m/s solo. — [Bishop, Halsey & Askew 2025, Biol Lett 21:20250031, PMID 40628301](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2025.0031)

### Inferences
- Suggested bounds: VMIN 6 m/s (keep), CRUISE 10–12 m/s, VMAX about 18–20 m/s. At the current 15 m/s cap, about 4 of the 21 Rome flocks would be impossible. Because speed in turns is nearly constant (Attanasi), the speed cap should not be what limits turning.
- Bruderer & Boldt 2001 (Ibis 143:178) is the standard radar reference, but its starling value was not retrieved. Pennycuick's 17.3 m/s for migrants is the verified substitute.

### Gaps
- Maximum level speed and dive or escape speed for starlings: none found. Hedenström & Rosén 2001 ([Behav Ecol 12:150](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/12.2.150)) frames escape by climbing, horizontal speeding and diving, but the full text was paywalled and no starling numbers were retrieved.
- Maximum-range speed (Vmr) for starlings was not given explicitly in any retrieved source.

## 3. Manoeuvrability: lateral acceleration, load factor, turn radius, roll, longitudinal acceleration, climb

### Takeaway
No direct measurement of a starling's maximum lateral acceleration or load factor was found. The best flock-turning proxy is jackdaws (about 2.5–3× a starling's mass): in collective turns their peak centripetal acceleration was about 7 m/s², nearly the same in every turn, and they slowed down to tighten turns. Slow-flying pigeons briefly generate up to 4.5 times body weight within a single downstroke. The model's 8 m/s² cap is therefore realistic for routine flock turns, but conservative for brief escape manoeuvres.

### Cited Findings
- **PROXY, jackdaw (*Corvus monedula*; ~200–250 g, my estimate, not given on the page), 21 flocking events of 4–196 birds, 3-D stereo:** peak radial (centripetal) acceleration "remains nearly constant at about **7 m s⁻²**" (about 70 % of body weight). "All flocks reduced their speed during turns", with minimum group speed down to 1.7 m/s and minimum group turn radius down to **0.6 m**. Minimum speed falls as 1/θ_g (θ_g is the group turning angle). Initial group speeds were 1.8–11.8 m/s. → SIM: an 8 m/s² cap matches the jackdaw value. Because turning is limited by acceleration, tight turns need lower speed (R = v²/a), so let birds slow when large heading changes are demanded. — [Ling et al. 2019, J R Soc Interface 16:20190450, PMID 31640502](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2019.0450)
- **PROXY, pigeon (319 ± 33 g, n = 3), slow 90° level turns at 3.3 ± 0.2 m/s:** net aerodynamic force averaged 1.33 ± 0.07 times body weight over the wingbeat. It peaked at **4.5 ± 0.4 BW in downstroke** and 2.3 ± 0.3 BW in upstroke, so the upstroke produced about 50 % of the downstroke force. Wing-beat frequency was 8.3 ± 0.3 Hz. Accumulated roll was 143 ± 16° (continuous) and 77 ± 14° (net per wingbeat). Within each wingbeat, roll oscillated by 13 ± 6° and yaw by 4 ± 3°. → SIM: mean load factor in a manoeuvre is about 1.3–1.5 g. Instantaneous peaks are much higher but average out over each roughly 0.1 s wing beat, so a point model should cap the wing-beat-averaged acceleration. — [Ros et al. 2011, PNAS 108:19990, PMID 22123982](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1107519108)
- **PROXY, pigeon flocks:** in flocks pigeons "engage in banked turns that double g-force". → SIM: brief turns of about 2 g are plausible in flocks. At 2 g the lateral component is √(2²−1)·g ≈ 17 m/s², which bounds an escape-mode cap. — [Usherwood et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10164) (search abstract; the bank angle itself was not retrieved)
- Starling take-off escape: groups of heavier starlings were less manoeuvrable. Mass manipulations mainly affected trajectory (angle of ascent) rather than velocity or distance covered. Loaded birds "defend" escape velocity at the expense of rate of climb. → SIM: in an escape, keep speed gain and let climb angle absorb any extra cost. — [Witter, Cuthill & Bonser 1994, Anim Behav 48:201](https://doi.org/10.1006/anbe.1994.1227) (abstract only)
- StarEscape and StarDisplay escape manoeuvre timings for starlings, used as model inputs: zig sideways 0.25 s, zig back 0.30 s, speed-up-forward 0.5 s, refractory period 1.0 s. — [Hemelrijk, van Zuidam & Hildenbrandt 2015, Behav Ecol Sociobiol 69:755](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3)
- **PROXY, zebra finch (~13 g):** maximum wind-tunnel speed fell from 14 to 12 m/s when birds carried implants. This shows how close small passerines operate to their performance limits. — [Tobalske, Puccinelli & Sheridan 2005, J Exp Biol 208:2895](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.01734)

### Inferences
- Minimum turn radius at cruise: with a_lat = 8 m/s² and v = 11.5 m/s, R = v²/a ≈ 16.5 m and turn rate ≈ 0.70 rad/s (40°/s). At 7 m/s² (jackdaw) R ≈ 19 m. For a 90° heading change the simplest model gives about 2.2 s, which fits Attanasi's 1.8–12.9 s collective turn durations.
- Consider two acceleration caps: about 8 m/s² for routine flight, and about 15–17 m/s² (≈2 g load factor) for at most about 0.3–0.5 s during alarm or escape.
- Making birds slow in sharp turns, as jackdaws do, is a cheap change toward realism. Starlings in large-radius flock turns, however, keep their speed (Attanasi 2014/2015), so apply the slowdown only when a bird is asked for a turn sharper than it can make at its current speed.

### Gaps
- No direct starling measurements of maximum load factor, roll rate (deg/s), maximum bank angle, longitudinal acceleration or deceleration, or climb rate were found. The Taylor (Oxford) group's papers on bird turning were not reached in this session. Witter et al. 1994 and Lee et al. 1996 (Proc R Soc B 263:619, take-off in gravid starlings) contain take-off velocity and angle numbers, but only abstracts were reached.
- No budgerigar turning data was found in this session.

## 4. How turns are executed (bank-to-turn, roll–heading delay)

### Takeaway
Birds turn mainly by rotating the body so that the roughly body-fixed aerodynamic force points into the turn: bank-to-turn, or "steer like helicopters" at low speed. The rotation comes first. The flight path follows within the first one or two wing beats, and the bird then rolls back while the path continues to curve. Starling ABMs that bank birds during turns, rolling in faster than out, reproduce flock dynamics and waves of agitation.

### Cited Findings
- **PROXY, pigeon (319 g):** the net downstroke force stayed "in a fixed direction relative to the body throughout the turn". Early in the turn, body rotations mainly redirected that force and changed the trajectory: 70.1 ± 4.1 % and 64.4 ± 17.8 % of rotation in the first two wing beats. In the last two wing beats, rotations mainly occurred about the force vector (60.2 ± 5.6 % and 69.4 ± 2.3 %), restoring the forward-flight body posture without changing the path. → SIM: model turning as bank first, then lateral acceleration a_lat = g·tan(bank) (or L·sin(bank)/m). Roll back toward level near the end of the turn. — [Ros et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1107519108)
- **PROXY, pigeon:** roll and pitch torques come from reorienting wing trajectories, with the outside wing sweeping more anteriorly. They do not come from left–right asymmetry in wing speed, area or amplitude. → SIM: drawn wings can stay symmetric during banked turns. — [Ros et al. 2015, J Exp Biol 218:480, PMID 25452503](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.104141)
- **PROXY, pigeon:** gaze is stabilised by head saccades during turns. Head movements are decoupled from the continuously rotating body, and head saccades correlate with changes in flight trajectory. → SIM: perception during a turn is effectively sampled in saccade-like steps, which supports a discrete reaction or update delay. — [Ros & Biewener 2017, Front Neurosci 11:655, PMID 29249929](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00655)
- StarDisplay, the starling ABM: lift is perpendicular to the wings, so banking redirects lift sideways. The banking-control gains are w_β,in = 10 and w_β,out = 1, so birds roll into a turn about 10 times faster than they roll out. These gains were tuned "to match the roll rates and bank angles as observed in the videos of real flocks". A turn causes "loss of effective lift and altitude", and after the apex "banking is reduced ... while there is still a loss of altitude, by which horizontal speed increases". → SIM: make roll-in fast and roll-out slow (asymmetric relaxation rates rather than the single rate of 6 s⁻¹), and couple banked turns to a small height loss and speed gain. — [Hildenbrandt et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arq149) (via [arXiv 0908.2677](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0908.2677))
- In StarDisplay, waves of agitation arise as orientation (roll) waves, not density waves. The alignment weight for banking was 2 N against 1 N for heading. Damping of waves over time is best explained by a smaller maximum bank angle during repeated zig-zag escapes. → SIM: give birds a bank-angle state that neighbours copy. That is what makes the visible dark bands, so a wave rendering should be driven by the bank-angle field. — [Hemelrijk et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3); [Hemelrijk et al. 2019, Behav Ecol Sociobiol 73](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-019-2734-4) (abstract-level)
- Starling collective turns are nearly planar, and birds follow equal-radius (parallel) paths, not equal-angular-velocity paths. — [Attanasi et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2015.0319)

### Inferences
- Roll-to-heading delay: no direct number was found. In the pigeon data the trajectory changes in the first wing beat after the body rotates, which implies a lag of about one wing-beat period (≈0.1 s at 8–10 Hz). The current bank relaxation rate of 6 s⁻¹ (τ ≈ 0.17 s) is in that range. A more faithful model makes lateral acceleration a consequence of bank (bank → a_lat) rather than deriving bank from a_lat as the code does now.
- Coordinated-turn geometry at 8 m/s² lateral acceleration gives bank = atan(8/9.81) ≈ 39°. At 2 g it is 60°. The current ±63° clamp is consistent with brief 2 g turns.

### Gaps
- No measured starling roll rate (deg/s) or maximum bank angle was found. The StarDisplay paper says roll rates were matched to videos but, in the text retrieved, does not give the measured values.

## 5. Reaction and latency times, visual temporal resolution and visual field

### Takeaway
The only direct starling measurement is the laboratory startle latency of about 76 ms to light and 81 ms to sound. A neighbour distance of about 1.1 m divided by 76 ms gives about 14.5 m/s, matching observed wave speeds of about 13 m/s with no need for "chorus-line" anticipation. Following a persistent change in a neighbour's heading is much slower, about 0.3–0.4 s in pigeon GPS data. Starlings see almost all around: maximum binocular overlap is 43°, with a rear blind sector that varies by up to 42° with eye position. No direct starling flicker-fusion measurement was found. Small passerines reach 130–146 Hz.

### Cited Findings
- Starlings in an anechoic chamber had a mean startle reaction time of **76.38 ms to a 1 ms white-light flash** and **80.64 ms to a 3 ms tone burst**, measured with 10⁻⁴ s timing resolution. → SIM: the minimum sensorimotor delay for an alarm response is about 75–80 ms. — [Pomeroy & Heppner 1977, Anim Behav 25:720](https://doi.org/10.1016/0003-3472(77)90121-X) (abstract via [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/000334727790121X))
- StarDisplay and StarEscape used a reaction time of **0.076 s (SD 0.01 s)** citing Pomeroy & Heppner 1977, plus a cue-identification time of 0.05 s. Empirical wave speeds in starling flocks were **3.66–25.24 m/s (mean 13.4 m/s)**. The paper notes that 1.1 m (mean nearest-neighbour distance) ÷ 0.076 s = **14.5 m/s**, so "no long-range anticipation is needed", contrary to Potts' chorus-line idea. Two to seven closest neighbours suffice. Wave speed falls as reaction time and cue-identification time rise. → SIM: a per-bird reaction delay of 76 ± 10 ms on copying a neighbour's alarm or escape will reproduce realistic wave speeds without long-range rules. — [Hemelrijk et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-1891-3)
- The original StarDisplay used a reaction time of **50 ms** with a 5 ms integration step. This may be where a "~50 ms" figure comes from; it is a model parameter, not a measurement. — [Hildenbrandt et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arq149)
- In turning starling flocks, directional information propagated linearly in time with negligible attenuation at **20–40 m/s**, while the flock flew at 7–12 m/s. Propagation speed obeys c_s = (1/βχ)·a/(1−Φ), so it is faster in more polarised flocks. → SIM: the propagation speed is a validation target. With neighbour spacing of about 1 m, 20–40 m/s implies an effective per-neighbour relay time of about 25–50 ms. That is shorter than the 76 ms startle latency, and the paper explains it through inertial (second-order) dynamics, not reflexes. — [Attanasi et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3035)
- **PROXY, jackdaw:** turning information propagated at 2.7–7.5 m/s in mobbing flocks. — [Ling et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2019.0450)
- **PROXY, homing pigeons (13 birds, flocks of 7–10, 32 h of 5 Hz GPS):** the mean delay between one bird's change of direction and another's copy was **0.37 ± 0.27 s** (C_min = 0.5) and **0.32 ± 0.20 s** (C_min = 0.9). The authors read these as reaction times for "following a persistent change in the direction of motion of neighbouring birds (rather than ... the considerably shorter reflex-like reactions of a startle response)". The delays formed a hierarchy, and leaders tended to fly in front. → SIM: use two latencies. About 76 ms for alarm or startle copying, and about 0.3 s effective lag (or an equivalent alignment time constant) for routine heading alignment. — [Nagy et al. 2010, Nature 464:890, PMID 20376149](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08891) (text via [arXiv 1010.5394](https://arxiv.org/abs/1010.5394))
- **Chorus-line claim:** Potts 1984 proposed that birds anticipate an approaching manoeuvre wave, so that propagation outpaces individual reaction time. Pomeroy & Heppner 1992 analysed the structure of turning in rock-dove flocks. → SIM: treat any "~15 ms" neighbour latency as unsupported; see Gaps. — [Potts 1984, Nature 309:344](https://doi.org/10.1038/309344a0); [Pomeroy & Heppner 1992, Auk 109:256](https://doi.org/10.2307/4088194) (citations verified from reference lists; contents **not retrieved**)
- Starling visual field: with the eyes fully converged, **maximum binocular overlap is 43°, about 40° below the bill**. Eye-movement amplitude is 11–32°. Optic-axis divergence ranges from 104° to 146°, so the rear blind sector and the frontal binocular field can each change by up to 42°. → SIM: a rear blind sector of a few tens of degrees is defensible. StarDisplay used a rear "blind angle" of 2 × 45° = 90° for cohesion and alignment, which is a modelling choice and probably larger than the anatomical blind area. — [Martin 1986, J Comp Physiol A 159:545](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00604174) (numbers from the search-result summary of the paper); [Hildenbrandt et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arq149)
- Flicker fusion, **PROXY small passerines:** blue tit (11 g) about 130 Hz (max 131), collared flycatcher (14 g) 128 ± 9.8 Hz (n = 7, max 141), pied flycatcher (15 g) 138 ± 6.5 Hz (n = 8, max 146). Comparisons cited in the same paper: pigeon about 75 Hz behaviourally (about 140 Hz by ERG), chicken 87–90 Hz, budgerigar 69 Hz. → SIM: visual sampling (about 7–10 ms) is far faster than the reaction time and can be ignored. The 60 fps render rate is below bird temporal resolution but that does not matter inside the model. — [Boström et al. 2016, PLoS ONE 11:e0151099, PMID 26990087](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0151099)
- Starling-specific flicker work states that birds generally have CFF above 100 Hz, but measured corticosterone responses to lighting flicker rather than CFF. — [Maddocks, Goldsmith & Cuthill 2001, Gen Comp Endocrinol, PMID 11742515](https://doi.org/10.1006/gcen.2001.7718) (abstract only)

### Inferences
- About 76 ms startle latency combined with about 1 m spacing reproduces the about 13–15 m/s agitation waves (Hemelrijk). The faster 20–40 m/s turning information measured by Attanasi requires inertial heading dynamics rather than a shorter reflex.
- Adding a 76 ms delay buffer per bird for alarm copying is cheap: about 5 frames at 60 fps.

### Gaps
- The "Pomeroy & Heppner ~15 ms chorus line" figure could not be verified. The 15 ms figure is probably Potts 1984 (dunlin), not Pomeroy & Heppner, but Potts' text was not retrieved. Treat any ~15 ms neighbour latency as **UNVERIFIED**. The verified Pomeroy & Heppner number is 76 ms (1977, startle).
- The "Attanasi 2014 ~50 ms" figure was not found in Attanasi 2014; the retrieved text gives no reaction time in ms. The only ~50 ms value found is StarDisplay's reaction-time parameter (Hildenbrandt 2010), which may be the source. Treat as **UNVERIFIED** as a measurement.
- No study measured a starling's flicker-fusion frequency directly.

## 6. Statistics of individual heading and speed fluctuations (autocorrelation, persistence, inertia)

### Takeaway
Starling 3-D data show four things. Turning information travels undamped, which requires behavioural inertia (second-order heading dynamics). Speed fluctuations are small yet correlated across the whole flock, which is best explained by "marginal" speed control: small deviations are tolerated and large ones are suppressed hard. Turns start at the elongated edges of the flock, where birds deviate more often and more persistently. Pigeon GPS gives heading-follow lags of about 0.3–0.4 s. No published value of individual heading autocorrelation time was found.

### Cited Findings
- The dissipationless spreading of turning information, with propagation speed tied to polarisation, cannot come from overdamped (Vicsek-type) alignment. It requires a conserved "spin" (behavioural inertia χ) in the heading dynamics. The paper gives no numeric value for χ or the relaxation times. → SIM: replace first-order heading alignment with an inertial-spin update: torque from neighbours drives angular velocity, which then turns the velocity. Alternatively, put inertia on a turn-rate state. — [Attanasi et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3035)
- Spontaneous turns begin with birds at the elongated tips or edges of the flock. Those birds deviate from the mean direction much more often, which shows that "persistent localized fluctuations" trigger collective turns. Measured over 1 s before a turn: farther along the lateral elongation axis means more frequent consistent fluctuations. → SIM: persistent rather than white noise is supported. A position-dependent noise amplitude, larger at the edges, is a supported refinement. — [Attanasi et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2015.0319)
- Speed fluctuations in starling flocks are correlated over the whole flock, yet individual speed fluctuations stay moderate. The model that reconciles both has **marginal speed confinement**: a potential with zero second derivative at the reference speed, which ignores small deviations and "ferociously" suppresses large ones. This was tested on flocks spanning more than two orders of magnitude in size. → SIM: the cubic speed term (W_SPD4·ds³) already in the model is this mechanism; keep it, and keep the quadratic term weak. — [Cavagna et al. 2022, Nat Commun 13, PMID 35538068](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29883-4); [Bialek et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1324045111)
- **PROXY, pigeon GPS (~400 g):** pairwise heading-follow delays of 0.32–0.37 s (SD 0.20–0.27 s). Leader–follower networks were hierarchical in 12 of 15 flights; 3 of 15 contained loops, and 99 ± 3 % of edges pointed consistently. Birds responded faster to neighbours seen mainly through the left eye. In the displayed correlation functions, C_ij(τ) stays at 0.8 or above at ±2 s, which suggests heading persistence of several seconds in flock flight. → SIM: with the current OU noise τ = 4 s, heading persistence on the scale of seconds is consistent. — [Nagy et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08891)
- **PROXY, pigeons:** further work on interaction rules and on speed versus leadership exists. → not retrieved: [Pettit et al. 2013, J R Soc Interface 10:20130529](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2013.0529); [Nagy et al. 2013 PNAS (context-dependent hierarchies)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1305552110)

### Inferences
- The current OU noise with τ = 4 s is a reasonable form for persistent behavioural fluctuations. No source gives a measured individual τ, so τ should be tuned against emergent observables: the Rome flock speeds and correlation lengths, the 1.8–12.9 s turn durations, and the 20–40 m/s information speed.
- Inertial heading dynamics, which Attanasi identified as the empirically required model change, is more important than fine-tuning τ.

### Gaps
- No published individual heading or speed autocorrelation times (s) for starlings or pigeons were found. Mora et al. 2016 ("Local equilibrium in bird flocks", Nat Phys) and Cavagna's inertial-spin papers may contain inferred dynamical parameters but were not retrieved. Ling and Ouellette's 3-D tracking covers jackdaws, not starlings; no starling-specific Ouellette dataset was found.

## 7. Energetics relevant to murmuration duration; why flying in flocks matters

### Takeaway
A starling in flight uses about 10–15 W, roughly 125–185 W/kg at 80 g, rising with speed over 6–14 m/s. Core temperature rises 2–4 °C during sustained flight. Whether flying in a flock costs or saves energy is contested: pigeon wing-beat data suggested a higher cost, and 2025 heart-rate data suggest a lower one.

### Cited Findings
- Metabolic power in two starlings rose from 10.4 to 14.9 W over 6.3–14.4 m/s. Flight-muscle efficiency was 0.13–0.23, and an efficiency of 0.18 is recommended for birds of about 0.1 kg. → SIM: about 12 W at cruise. A 20-minute display costs roughly 12 W × 1200 s ≈ 14 kJ (my calculation). — [Ward et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.204.19.3311)
- Three independent methods (heat-transfer modelling, doubly labelled water and mask respirometry; 4 starlings) agreed when efficiency was assumed to be 0.11–0.19. Respirometry and heat-transfer power rose linearly with speed, while doubly labelled water gave a U-shaped curve. — [Ward et al. 2004, J Exp Biol 207:4291, PMID 15531650](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.01281)
- Heat loss at 10.2 m/s averaged 11.3 W (n = 4): 81 % by convection and 8 % by radiation, with 55 % lost from the wings. — [Ward et al. 1999, J Exp Biol 202:1589, PMID 10333506](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.202.12.1589)
- Flight-muscle efficiency averaged 15–18 %, so the metabolic power curve is shallower than models predict. Rayner also proposed that birds may not perceive cost as a smooth function of airspeed. → SIM: speed choice near Vmp is weakly constrained energetically, which supports a broad speed distribution. — [Rayner 1999, J Exp Biol 202:3449, PMID 10562528](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.202.23.3449)
- During 30-minute wind-tunnel flights, core temperature was 42.7–44.0 °C, 2–4 °C above resting, and independent of ambient temperature from 0 to 28 °C. In 90-minute flights, starlings dehydrated at all air temperatures above 7 °C. → SIM: this is context only, but it shows that 30–90 minutes of continuous flight is within starling capability. — [Torre-Bueno 1976, J Exp Biol 65:471](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.65.2.471); [Torre-Bueno 1978, J Exp Biol 75:231](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.75.1.231)
- **PROXY, pigeon:** flock birds flapped faster (6.6–7.0 Hz vs 5.5 Hz solo) and made banked turns that double g-force, and were concluded to pay a cost. — [Usherwood et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10164)
- **PROXY, pigeon (0.34–0.48 kg; 32 birds instrumented, 20 usable ECG loggers):** the 2025 re-analysis found that in flocks wing-beat frequency rose by only about 0.1 Hz while vertical body amplitude fell by about 0.45 mm (from about 13.3 mm). Body mechanical power was therefore 2.403 W/kg in flocks against 2.447 W/kg solo. Heart rate was 652 ± 7.4 beats/min in flocks against 621 ± 5.4 solo. But flocks flew faster (18.6 vs 15.2 m/s) and homed sooner, so they used **14.4 % fewer total heartbeats**, an estimated 10–12 % lower total energy. → SIM: the energetic argument for flocking stays open, so the model should not encode an aerodynamic benefit or penalty for close flight. — [Bishop, Halsey & Askew 2025](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2025.0031)

### Inferences
- At about 12 W, a 30-minute murmuration costs about 22 kJ, roughly 0.55 g of fat at 39 kJ/g. That is about 0.7 % of an 80 g bird's mass. These are my calculations, not sourced. The cost is modest but not trivial in winter, which fits murmurations being short (tens of minutes) and varying from day to day.

### Gaps
- No direct measurements of murmuration duration or of energetic cost during murmurations were found in this session; that is likely covered by another researcher. Metabolic cost of turning or manoeuvring flight in starlings was not found.
